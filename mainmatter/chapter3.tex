\section{Introduction}
The evaluation of beam-coupling impedance and wakefields are critical in the design 
and operation of particle accelerators. As charged particle beams traverse accelerator 
structures, interactions with geometric discontinuities and material inhomogeneities 
generate \gls{em} fields---known as wakefields---that can degrade beam stability, cause 
beam-induced heating, and limit machine performance \cite{metral_impedance_2020}. The 
frequency-domain counterpart of these interactions is the beam-coupling impedance, an 
intrinsic property of each component used to quantify wakefield effects.

Analytical methods provide accurate impedance predictions for simplified structures such 
as resistive walls \cite{yokoya_resistive_1993, migliorati_resistive_2019}, RF cavities 
\cite{hofmann_analytic_1990, jensen_rf_2014}, and transitions such as tapers or steps 
\cite{yokoya_impedance_1990, stupakov_low_2007, palumbo_wake_1995}. However, for 
realistic accelerator components with complex geometries, the full three-dimensional 
solution of Maxwell's equations \cite{maxwell_viii_1997} is required.

Maxwell's equations can be solved numerically in either frequency or time domain, and 
several discretization approaches have been developed. Among the most widely used 
methods are the \gls{fdtd} method, the \gls{fem}, and the \gls{fvtd} method. These 
techniques differ primarily in the formulation of the governing equations (differential 
or weak form), the choice of basis functions, and the handling of material interfaces 
and boundary conditions.

In the frequency domain, for lossless and source-free structures, the problem reduces 
to an eigenmode formulation, as used in CST Eigenmode, HFSS, or ACE3P's Omega3P. In 
contrast, time-domain solvers offer full-wave impedance characterization by directly 
computing Maxwell's Equations in integral (see Eqs.~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:a}--\eqref{eq:Maxwell:d}) or differential form. Commercial solvers such as 
CST Wakefield, GDFiDL, and closed-source ECHO3D~\cite{zagorodnov_echo4d} and 
PBCI~\cite{Lau:1182975} report to use the \gls{fit} \cite{weiland_finite_2003}, while 
ACE3P's T3P employs the \gls{fem} \cite{candel_ace3p_2010}.

Several 3D open-source solvers implement full-wave time-domain methods, such as Meep 
\cite{oskooi_meep_2010} and WarpX \cite{fedeli_pushing_2022}, both based on the 
\gls{fdtd} method \cite{yee_numerical_1966}, and EMcLaW \cite{moreno_emclaw_2021}, 
which uses a \gls{fvtd} Godunov scheme. However, these tools lack key features needed 
for beam-coupling impedance calculations---such as native \gls{cad} geometry support, 
advanced material modeling, or wakefield-tailored post-processing.

\subsection{Motivation for the Finite Integration Technique}

The \gls{fit}, originally introduced by Weiland, discretizes the integral form of 
Maxwell's equations on a pair of staggered primal and dual grids. Unlike approaches 
that discretize differential operators directly, \gls{fit} assigns physical \glspl{dof} to geometric objects---edge-integrated electric quantities and face-integrated 
magnetic fluxes---and constructs discrete curl and divergence operators that preserve 
the fundamental topological identities of the continuous equations exactly at the 
discrete level. This separation between metric-free topological operators and metric- 
and material-dependent matrices provides a transparent framework for incorporating 
complex materials, boundary conditions, and time-dependent sources.

In its time-domain formulation, \gls{fit} leads to a leap-frog scheme that is 
equivalent in accuracy to the classical Yee \gls{fdtd} algorithm, while offering a 
clearer algebraic structure derived systematically from integral balance laws on a 
primal--dual cell complex. This structure is particularly well suited to wakefield and 
impedance computations in accelerator components with arbitrary three-dimensional 
geometry and inhomogeneous materials, where robustness and computational efficiency are 
essential.

Historically, \gls{fit} was first applied to accelerator problems in the MAFIA solver 
and later became the foundation of the CST transient solver. Related time-domain 
codes---ECHO and GdfidL---exploit the same staggered-grid philosophy, emphasising 
conservation, sparse operators, and explicit time stepping. In this thesis, \gls{fit} 
is adopted as the core numerical method of \wakis, enabling broadband wakefield and 
impedance characterisation from a single time-domain simulation.

In the following sections, we introduce the primal and dual grids used in \wakis\ and 
define the discrete \gls{em} degrees of freedom associated with edges and faces. 
Applying discrete versions of Stokes' and Gauss' theorems converts the integral 
Maxwell equations into the \glspl{mge}, which constitute the algebraic core of the 
solver and serve as the starting point for implementing boundary conditions, sources, 
and the explicit leap-frog time-advancing scheme.

\section{Description of Maxwell Grid Equations}
\label{sec:mge}

\subsection{Integral form of Maxwell's equations}
\label{sec:mge_integral}

The ultimate goal is to solve Maxwell's equations in a box-shaped simulation domain 
that includes arbitrarily 3D-shaped material regions, under certain boundary
conditions, and under the excitation of a passing beam source. To achieve this, we 
start from the most general approach, the integral form of Maxwell's equations.

Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^3$ denote the computational domain with boundary 
$\partial\Omega$ and outward unit normal $\mathbf{n}$. In \gls{fit}, the \gls{em} 
field evolution is formulated starting from the general integral form of Maxwell 
equations, written for an arbitrary oriented surface $A$ with line boundary $\partial 
A$ and an arbitrary volume $V$ with surface boundary $\partial V$:

\begin{align}
\oint_{\partial A} \mathbf{E}\cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{s} &= -\iint_{A}\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}\cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{A} \label{eq:Maxwell:a}\\[6pt]
\oint_{\partial A} \mathbf{H}\cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{s} &= \iint_{A}\left(\frac{\partial \mathbf{D}}{\partial t} + \mathbf{J}\right)\cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{A} \label{eq:Maxwell:b}\\[6pt]
\oiint_{\partial V} \mathbf{B}\cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{A} &= 0 \label{eq:Maxwell:c}\\[6pt]
\oiint_{\partial V} \mathbf{D}\cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{A} &= \iiint_{V}\rho\, \mathrm{d}V \label{eq:Maxwell:d}
\end{align}

Equations~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:a}--\eqref{eq:Maxwell:d} represent the evolution of the 
electric field $\mathbf{E}$, magnetic flux density $\mathbf{B}$, electric displacement 
field $\mathbf{D}$, and magnetic field intensity $\mathbf{H}$, in the presence of a 
current density $\mathbf{J}$ and charge density $\rho$. Taken together, they govern all 
classical electromagnetic phenomena and form the foundation of the numerical method 
developed in this work:

\begin{mydescription}
    \item[Faraday's law~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:a}:] the circulation of the electric 
    field $\mathbf{E}$ around the closed boundary $\partial A$ of any oriented surface $A$ 
    equals the negative rate of change of the magnetic flux through that surface. This 
    relation captures the fundamental coupling between time-varying magnetic fields and 
    induced electric fields.

    \item[Amp\`ere--Maxwell law~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:b}:] the circulation of the 
    magnetic field intensity $\mathbf{H}$ around $\partial A$ is driven by two 
    contributions --- the conduction current density $\mathbf{J}$ and the displacement 
    current $\partial \mathbf{D}/\partial t$, the latter introduced by Maxwell to ensure 
    consistency with charge conservation. These source terms enable the propagation of 
    electromagnetic waves even in the absence of free charges.

    \item[Gauss's law for magnetic fields~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:c}:] the net magnetic 
    flux through any closed surface $\partial V$ is identically zero, expressing the 
    absence of magnetic monopoles. This constraint is satisfied by construction in the 
    discrete setting when the initial conditions are divergence-free.

    \item[Gauss's law for electric fields~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:d}:] the total electric 
    flux through a closed surface $\partial V$ equals the net free charge enclosed in the 
    volume $V$. Similarly to Eq.~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:c}, this relation is automatically 
    preserved at the discrete level by the consistency of the primal--dual grid 
    construction.
\end{mydescription}

The constitutive relations close the system by linking the field intensities 
$(\mathbf{E},\mathbf{H})$ to the flux densities $(\mathbf{D},\mathbf{B})$. In the 
most general (possibly anisotropic) case,
\begin{align}
\mathbf{D} &= \underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}\,\mathbf{E}, \qquad
\mathbf{B} = \underline{\underline{\mu}}\,\mathbf{H}, \qquad
\mathbf{J} = \underline{\underline{\sigma}}\,\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{s}},
\label{eq:constitutive_continuum}
\end{align}

The constitutive relations in Eq.~\eqref{eq:constitutive_continuum} relate these 
fields through the material properties: permittivity $\varepsilon$, permeability 
$\mu$, and conductivity $\sigma$. They are represented as $\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}$, 
$\underline{\underline{\mu}}$ and $\underline{\underline{\sigma}}$, which are (in 
general space-dependent) second-order tensors, and $\mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{s}}$ denotes 
impressed sources such as the particle beam current introduced in 
Sec.~\ref{sec:beam_source}.

\subsection{The computational grid $G$} 

\begin{figure}[!tbh]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/3.1_computational_grid.pdf}
    \caption{Schematic of the simulation domain discretization. The domain is 
    considered as a rectangular box subdivided into individual computational cells 
    composing the Cartesian grid. Each computational cell $n$ is ordered using the 
    lexicographic indexing convention.}
    \label{fig:comp_grid}
\end{figure}

In order to numerically solve the presented set of equations, the computational domain $\Omega$ is discretized into a set number of hexahedral computational cells in each coordinate direction $x$, $y$, $z$, forming a computational grid $\mathbf{G}$ of $\{N_x \times N_y \times N_z\}$ cells. The grid $G$ is defined in the orthogonal coordinate system 
$\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}(x,y,z)$ as:

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathbf{G} = \{ (x_i,\, y_j,\, z_k)\quad 
    x_0 < x_i < x_I,\ i = 1,\ldots,I-1\quad \\
    y_0 < y_j < y_J,\ j = 1,\ldots,J-1\quad \\
    z_0 < z_k < z_K,\ k = 1,\ldots,K-1 \}
\end{split}
\label{eq:grid}
\end{equation}

where $I=N_x$, $J=N_y$, and $K=N_z$. From this spatial construction of the grid, all 
grid nodes $n$ of each computational cell in $\mathbf{G}$ are numbered linearly 
following the lexicographic indexing convention (see \autoref{def:Lexicographic_indexing}):

\begin{equation}
    n = iM_x + jM_y + kM_z,
    \qquad n = 0,\ldots,N = IJK-1.
\end{equation}

The quantities $M_x,\ M_y,\ M_z$ can be chosen to follow different ordering 
conventions. In the original \gls{fit} formulation \cite{weiland_wake_1992}, the 
choice $M_x = 1,\ M_y = I,\ M_z = IJ$ sets a Fortran-order scheme, which is also 
followed in this thesis.

\begin{definition}{Lexicographic indexing}{Lexicographic_indexing}
A bijective mapping from multi-dimensional grid indices $(i, j, k)$ to a single 
linear index $n \in [0,\, N_x N_y N_z)$. Two standard conventions exist: 
\textbf{Fortran-order} (column-major), where the first index varies fastest,
$n = i + N_x j + N_x N_y k$, and \textbf{C-order} (row-major), where the last 
index varies fastest, $n = k + N_z j + N_z N_y i$. The choice of ordering 
convention directly affects the sparsity pattern of the \gls{fit} matrices and has 
implications for memory access efficiency and solver performance.
\end{definition}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:comp_grid}, each computational cell $n$ is identified by 
an index triplet $\{i,\ j,\ k\}$, where $i$ represents the index along the 
$x$-direction, $j$ along the $y$-direction, and $k$ along the $z$-direction. The 
resulting ordered set of indices allows the field information to be stored in arrays 
and the matrix operators that numerically replace the continuous integral operators to 
be constructed. 

\subsection{The computational cell $n$}

\begin{figure}[!tbh]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/3.2_computational_cell.pdf}
    \caption{Schematic of the quantities allocation of each computational cell $n$.}
    \label{fig:comp_cell}
\end{figure}

The field distribution in the volume of interest is replaced by discrete field values on
each computational grid cell $n$ cell. Rather than assigning all six field components to grid nodes (which causes problems at material interfaces where some components are discontinuous), the components of $\mathbf{E}$ are placed at the midpoints of cell edges $L= l_x, \ l_y, \ 
l_z$, and the components of the magnetic field $\mathbf{H}$ pierce the center of each cell face $A = A_x, \ A_y, \ A_z$. Figure~\ref{fig:comp_cell} shows a schematic of the base computational cell $n$, together with the field component locations and their indexation.

\subsubsection*{Cell geometry}

Each computational cell $n$, identified by the index triplet $(i,j,k)$, is
fully described by its three edge lengths and three face areas. The edge lengths
are the distances between adjacent grid nodes along each coordinate direction:
\begin{equation}
    l_{x,n} = x_{i+1} - x_i, \qquad
    l_{y,n} = y_{j+1} - y_j, \qquad
    l_{z,n} = z_{k+1} - z_k.
    \label{eq:edge_lengths}
\end{equation}
The face area perpendicular to each coordinate axis is the product of the two
transverse edge lengths:
\begin{equation}
    A_{x,n} = l_{y,n}\,l_{z,n}, \qquad
    A_{y,n} = l_{z,n}\,l_{x,n}, \qquad
    A_{z,n} = l_{x,n}\,l_{y,n}.
    \label{eq:face_areas}
\end{equation}
These six scalar quantities per cell carry all the metric information needed to
approximate the line and surface integrals in Maxwell's equations. They are
assembled into two $3N \times 3N$ diagonal matrices, where $N = N_x N_y N_z$ is
the total number of cells and the factor of three accounts for the three spatial
components of each field quantity:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{D}_s = \mathrm{Diag}
        \!\left(l_{x,1},\ldots,l_{x,N},\;
                l_{y,1},\ldots,l_{y,N},\;
                l_{z,1},\ldots,l_{z,N}\right),
    \label{eq:Ds}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{D}_A = \mathrm{Diag}
        \!\left(A_{x,1},\ldots,A_{x,N},\;
                A_{y,1},\ldots,A_{y,N},\;
                A_{z,1},\ldots,A_{z,N}\right).
    \label{eq:Da}
\end{equation}
$\mathbf{D}_s$ will weight the line integrals of $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{H}$
along cell edges, while $\mathbf{D}_A$ will weight the flux integrals of
$\mathbf{B}$ and $\mathbf{D}$ through cell faces. Both matrices appear explicitly
in the Maxwell Grid Equations derived in Sec.~\ref{sec:mge_final}.

\subsubsection*{Field state vectors}

Once the field components are allocated on the grid, they are assembled into
global $3N$-vectors following the lexicographic ordering introduced in
Sec.~\ref{sec:mge_integral}:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathbf{e} &= (E_{x,1},\ldots,E_{x,N},\;
                       E_{y,1},\ldots,E_{y,N},\;
                       E_{z,1},\ldots,E_{z,N})^t, \\[4pt]
        \mathbf{h} &= (H_{x,1},\ldots,H_{x,N},\;
                       H_{y,1},\ldots,H_{y,N},\;
                       H_{z,1},\ldots,H_{z,N})^t.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:field_vectors}
\end{equation}
These two vectors fully describe the electromagnetic state of the system at any
given time. All spatial operations in the FIT framework --- curl, divergence, and
their duals --- are expressed as matrix--vector products acting on $\mathbf{e}$
and $\mathbf{h}$, replacing the continuous differential operators of Maxwell's
equations.

\subsubsection*{A closer look at \glspl{dof} indexation}
Notice the field indexes shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:comp_cell}. When moving in the x direction, the lexicographic index $n$ increases by 1. Therefore the field components $E_{z, n+1}$ and $E_{y, n+1}$ would already belong to the next computational cell $(n+1)$. Note the described lengths $l_x,n^*, \ l_y,n^*, \ l_z,n^*,$, denoted with a star in the figure. The indexes would be correct if we use a uniformly spaced grid in all three dimensions. However, if different edge lengths per dimension are included in the numerical method, the right indexing for each length would be $l_x,n+N_xN_y , \ l_y,n+N_xN_y$ since they would belong to the next cell in z-direction ($n+N_xN_y$) and  $l_z,n+N_x,$, since this edge length would belong to the next cell in y-direction ($n+N_x$).

\subsection{Primal--dual cell staggering}
\label{sec:mge_yee}

\begin{figure}[!tbh]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/3.3_yee_grid.pdf}
    \caption{Pair of Yee-staggered primal and dual cells, showing the \gls{dof} allocation}
    \label{fig:yee_cell}
\end{figure}

The placement of electromagnetic field components in the cell discretization shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:comp_cell} is not arbitrary. Introduced by Yee~\cite{yee_numerical_1966} in 1966, the staggered-cell arrangement assigns electric and magnetic degrees of freedom to complementary geometric objects -- edges and faces -- of two interlocking grids offset by half a cell in each coordinate direction. This spatial staggering is the key ingredient that confers the scheme its numerical stability, second-order accuracy, and the ability to satisfy the divergence constraints of Maxwell's equations by construction, without any additional enforcement. In the Finite Integration Technique, this idea is elevated to a systematic algebraic framework by deriving the discrete operators directly from the integral form of Maxwell's equations on a \emph{primal--dual cell complex}. The schematic in Figure~\ref{fig:yee_cell} illustrates the Yee staggering. 

The special field allocation generates a second, dual grid $\tilde{\mathbf{G}}$, in
which the magnetic field components $\mathbf{H}$ are placed where the electric field
components $\mathbf{E}$ reside in $\mathbf{G}$, and vice versa: the $\mathbf{H}$
components pierce the centres of the primal faces, which coincide with the edges of the
dual cells, while the $\mathbf{E}$ components lie on the primal edges, which coincide
with the face centres of the dual cells. The dual grid is defined by:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{G}} = \left\{(\tilde{x}_i,\,\tilde{y}_j,\,\tilde{z}_k);\quad
        \tilde{x}_1 < \tilde{x}_i < \tilde{x}_I,\ i=2,\ldots,I-1;\ \ldots
    \right\},
\end{equation}
with dual node coordinates located at the midpoints of the primal cell edges:
\begin{align}
    \tilde{x}_i &= \frac{x_{i+1}+x_i}{2}, \quad 1 \le i \le I-1,
                    \qquad \tilde{x}_I = 0, \\
    \tilde{y}_j &= \frac{y_{j+1}+y_j}{2}, \quad 1 \le j \le J-1,
                    \qquad \tilde{y}_J = 0, \\
    \tilde{z}_k &= \frac{z_{k+1}+z_k}{2}, \quad 1 \le k \le K-1,
                    \qquad \tilde{z}_K = 0.
\end{align}
Notice how, due to the staggering, the last dual node coordinate must be set to zero.
The dual grid has one fewer interior node than the primal grid in each direction: while
the primal grid has $I$ nodes in $x$, the dual grid only has $I-1$ midpoints. The
boundary node $\tilde{x}_I$ therefore has no corresponding primal interval to bisect,
and is set to zero to indicate that it lies outside the computational domain. In this case, the selected boundary condition (see section \ref{}) takes over the role of closing the outermost dual cell.

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:yee_cell}, each dual cell $n$ is therefore centered on a primal node, and its geometry is described
by dual edge lengths and dual face areas defined in exact analogy with the primal
quantities. The dual edge lengths connecting adjacent dual nodes are:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{l}_{x,n} = \tilde{x}_{i+1} - \tilde{x}_i, \qquad
    \tilde{l}_{y,n} = \tilde{y}_{j+1} - \tilde{y}_j, \qquad
    \tilde{l}_{z,n} = \tilde{z}_{k+1} - \tilde{z}_k,
\end{equation}
and the dual face areas, perpendicular to each coordinate axis, are:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{A}_{x,n} = \tilde{l}_{y,n}\,\tilde{l}_{z,n}, \qquad
    \tilde{A}_{y,n} = \tilde{l}_{z,n}\,\tilde{l}_{x,n}, \qquad
    \tilde{A}_{z,n} = \tilde{l}_{x,n}\,\tilde{l}_{y,n}.
\end{equation}

Analogously to Eqs.~\ref{eq:Ds} and \ref{eq:Da}, the dual edge lengths and face areas are assembled into the corresponding dual diagonal matrices $\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_s$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_A$:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{D}}_s = \mathrm{Diag}
        \!\left(\tilde{l}_{x,1},\ldots,\tilde{l}_{x,N},\;
                \tilde{l}_{y,1},\ldots,\tilde{l}_{y,N},\;
                \tilde{l}_{z,1},\ldots,\tilde{l}_{z,N}\right),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{D}}_A = \mathrm{Diag}
        \!\left(\tilde{A}_{x,1},\ldots,\tilde{A}_{x,N},\;
                \tilde{A}_{y,1},\ldots,\tilde{A}_{y,N},\;
                \tilde{A}_{z,1},\ldots,\tilde{A}_{z,N}\right).
\end{equation}
The four matrices $\mathbf{D}_s$, $\mathbf{D}_A$, $\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_s$,
$\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_A$ together carry all the geometric information of the
primal--dual grid pair, and will appear in the Maxwell Grid Equations derived
in Sec.~\ref{sec:mge_final}.

\begin{definition}{Yee grid}{yee_grid}
A \emph{Yee grid} is a pair of staggered cell complexes -- a \emph{primal} grid $G$ and a \emph{dual} grid $\tilde{G}$ -- constructed on a structured orthogonal hexahedral partition of the computational domain $\Omega$. The \textbf{dual grid} is obtained by shifting the primal grid by half a cell in each coordinate direction, such that:
\begin{itemize}
  \item primal edges $L$ are pierced orthogonally by dual faces $\tilde{A}$,
  \item primal faces $A$ are pierced orthogonally by dual edges $\tilde{L}$,
  \item primal cell volumes $V$ centers correspond to dual grid nodes $\tilde{n}$ and vice versa.
\end{itemize}
Electric degrees of freedom are allocated on primal edges and dual faces; magnetic degrees of freedom on primal faces and dual edges. This interlocking structure ensures that the discrete curl and divergence operators satisfy the topological identities of Maxwell's equations exactly, independently of mesh resolution.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Curl $\mathbf{C}$ and divergence $\mathbf{S}$ operators }
\label{sec:mge_operators}

One of the principal advantages of the \gls{fit} formulation is that it discretizes
the integral form of Maxwell's equations (see Eqs. ~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:a}--\eqref{eq:Maxwell:d}) directly into a set of sparse matrix operators whose entries take only the values $+1$, $-1$, or $0$. Rather than implementing the field updates as nested loops over grid indices --- as is typical in conventional \gls{fdtd} implementations --- the entire spatial discretization is encoded in the topology matrices $\mathbf{C}$, $\tilde{\mathbf{C}}$, $\mathbf{S}$, and $\tilde{\mathbf{S}}$, which act directly on vectors of field degrees of freedom. This representation stays close to the underlying physics: each matrix row corresponds to a concrete geometric operation, either a circulation integral around a cell edge loop or a flux integral over a cell face, in direct correspondence with the integral laws from which it is derived. At the same time, expressing the spatial operators as sparse matrix--vector products exposes the full potential for computational efficiency, enabling vectorized and cache-friendly evaluation on modern hardware without any modification to the mathematical structure of the equations.

\subsubsection*{Curl operator}

\begin{figure}[!tbh]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/3.4_yee_curl.pdf}
    \caption{Schematic of the curl operation on a pair of Yee primal and dual cells,
             showing the field quantities involved.}
    \label{fig:yee_curl}
\end{figure}

The discrete curl operator is constructed by applying Faraday's law in integral form
to every face of each primal cell. Consider the $z$-directed face of primal cell $n$:
the circulation of $\mathbf{E}$ around its boundary involves the four tangential
electric field components on the surrounding edges. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:yee_curl}, for the $z$-component of the primal curl one collects the contributions with their correct signs:

\begin{equation}
    (\mathbf{C}\,\mathbf{e})_{z,n} =
        +E_{y,\,n+N_x N_y}
        - E_{y,\,n+N_x N_y+1}
        - E_{x,\,n+N_x N_y}
        + E_{x,\,n+N_x(N_y+1)},
\end{equation}

where $N_x = I$ and $N_y = J$ are the number of primal nodes in the $x$ and $y$
directions respectively, and the index offsets reflect the linear node numbering
$n = 1 + (i-1)N_x + (j-1)N_x N_y$. Repeating this construction for all three face
orientations and all $N$ cells yields the global primal curl matrix:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{C} =
    \begin{pmatrix}
         0          & -\mathbf{P}_z &  \mathbf{P}_y \\
         \mathbf{P}_z &  0          & -\mathbf{P}_x \\
        -\mathbf{P}_y &  \mathbf{P}_x &  0
    \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where the elementary difference matrices $\mathbf{P}_x$, $\mathbf{P}_y$,
$\mathbf{P}_z$ encode the $\pm 1$ connectivity pattern between adjacent nodes along
each coordinate direction (see Sec.~\ref{sec:mge_yee}).

The dual curl operator $\tilde{\mathbf{C}}$ is constructed in exactly the same way,
but now applied to the faces of the dual cells. Its role is to compute the circulation
of $\mathbf{H}$ around each dual face, as required by Amp\`ere's law. Reading the
signed field contributions from Fig.~\ref{fig:yee_curl}, the $x$-component of the
dual curl at dual cell $\tilde{n}$ is:
\begin{equation}
    (\tilde{\mathbf{C}}\,\mathbf{h})_{x,\tilde{n}} =
        + H_{y,\,n+N_x}
        - H_{y,\,n+N_x+1}
        - H_{x,\,n+1}
        + H_{x,\,n+N_x+1},
\end{equation}
where the $\mathbf{H}$ components are indexed on the primal grid according to the
staggering convention. A key algebraic property of the FIT discretization is that the
dual curl matrix is simply the transpose of the primal curl matrix:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{C}} = \mathbf{C}^t.
\end{equation}
This relation is not an approximation but an exact consequence of the primal--dual
topology: every primal edge is shared by exactly two dual faces, and every dual edge
is shared by exactly two primal faces, so the two circulation operators are each
other's adjoint.

\subsubsection*{Divergence operator}

\begin{figure}[!tbh]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/3.5_yee_divergence.pdf}
    \caption{Schematic of the divergence operation on a primal and dual cell.}
    \label{fig:yee_divergence}
\end{figure}

The discrete divergence operator is obtained by applying Gauss's law in integral form
to each cell, summing the signed normal flux components through all six faces. For
the primal cell $n$, the net outward flux of $\mathbf{H}$ through its faces involves
the six $\mathbf{H}$ components sitting at the face centres, as shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:yee_divergence}:
\begin{equation}
    (\mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{h})_n =
        - H_{x,n} + H_{x,\,n+1}
        - H_{y,n} + H_{y,\,n+N_x}
        - H_{z,n} + H_{z,\,n+N_x N_y}.
\end{equation}
Assembling this for all $N$ primal cells gives the primal divergence matrix:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{S} = \bigl(\mathbf{P}_x\;\big|\;\mathbf{P}_y\;\big|\;\mathbf{P}_z\bigr),
\end{equation}
which maps the $3N$-vector of $\mathbf{H}$ components to an $N$-vector of net fluxes,
one per primal cell. Enforcing $\mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{D}_A\,\mathbf{b} = 0$ ensures
that the discrete magnetic flux is divergence-free by construction.

The dual divergence operator $\tilde{\mathbf{S}}$ acts on the dual cells $\tilde{n}$
in the same fashion, now accumulating the net outward flux of $\mathbf{E}$ through
the six faces of each dual cell. From Fig.~\ref{fig:yee_divergence}:
\begin{equation}
    (\tilde{\mathbf{S}}\,\mathbf{e})_{\tilde{n}} =
        - E_{x,n} + E_{x,\,n+1}
        - E_{y,n} + E_{y,\,n+N_x}
        - E_{z,n} + E_{z,\,n+N_x N_y},
\end{equation}
yielding the dual divergence matrix:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{S}} =
        \bigl(\mathbf{P}_x^t\;\big|\;\mathbf{P}_y^t\;\big|\;\mathbf{P}_z^t\bigr) = S^t
\end{equation}
Enforcing $\tilde{\mathbf{S}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_A\,(\dot{\mathbf{d}}+\mathbf{j})=0$
expresses the discrete charge continuity equation on the dual grid.


\subsection{Maxwell Grid Equations}
\label{sec:mge_final}

With the topological operators $\mathbf{C}$, $\tilde{\mathbf{C}}$, $\mathbf{S}$,
$\tilde{\mathbf{S}}$ and the metric matrices $\mathbf{D}_s$, $\mathbf{D}_A$,
$\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_s$, $\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_A$ established in the previous sections,
all the ingredients are in place to derive the full set of \emph{Maxwell Grid
Equations} (MGE). The procedure is the same for each equation: the corresponding
integral Maxwell law is applied to a single cell, the integrals are approximated
by the discrete field values allocated on its edges and faces, and the metric weights
are separated from the topological $\pm 1$ connectivity to expose the matrix
structure.

\subsubsection*{Faraday's law $\longrightarrow$ Primal Curl Equation}

Applying Faraday's law~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:a} to the $z$-directed face of primal
cell $n$:
\begin{equation}
    \oint_{\partial A_{z,n}} \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{s}
    = -\iint_{A_{z,n}} \frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} \cdot d\mathbf{A}.
\end{equation}
The left-hand side is approximated by the four tangential $\mathbf{E}$ components
on the surrounding primal edges, each weighted by the corresponding edge length.
Reading the signed contributions from Fig.~\ref{fig:yee_curl}:
\begin{align}
    \oint_{\partial A_{z,n}} \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{s}
    &\approx
        + l_{x,\,n+N_x(N_y+1)}\, E_{x,\,n+N_x(N_y+1)}
        - l_{x,\,n+N_xN_y}\, E_{x,\,n+N_xN_y} \nonumber\\
    &\quad
        + l_{y,\,n+N_xN_y}\, E_{y,\,n+N_xN_y}
        - l_{y,\,n+N_xN_y+1}\, E_{y,\,n+N_xN_y+1},
\end{align}

while the right-hand side reduces to the single $\mathbf{B}$ component normal to
the face, weighted by the face area and expressed in terms of $\mathbf{h}$ via the
constitutive relation $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{D}_\mu\,\mathbf{h}$:
\begin{equation}
    \iint_{A_{z,n}} \frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} \cdot d\mathbf{A}
    \approx A_{z,n}\,\frac{\partial B_{z,n}}{\partial t}
    = A_{z,n}\,\mu_n\,\frac{\partial H_{z,n}}{\partial t}.
\end{equation}
Repeating this for all three face orientations and all $N$ primal cells, and
separating the topological $\pm 1$ pattern (encoded in $\mathbf{C}$) from the
edge lengths (encoded in $\mathbf{D}_s$) and face areas (encoded in $\mathbf{D}_A$),
yields the first Maxwell Grid Equation:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\mathbf{C}\,\mathbf{D}_s\,\mathbf{e}
        = -\mathbf{D}_A\,\mathbf{D}_\mu\,\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}}{\partial t}}
    \label{eq:MGE:faraday}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Amp\`ere--Maxwell law $\longrightarrow$ Dual Curl Equation}

Applying Amp\`ere's law~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:b} to the $x$-directed face of dual
cell $\tilde{n}$:
\begin{equation}
    \oint_{\partial \tilde{A}_{x,\tilde{n}}} \mathbf{H} \cdot d\mathbf{s}
    = \iint_{\tilde{A}_{x,\tilde{n}}}
        \frac{\partial \mathbf{D}}{\partial t} \cdot d\mathbf{A}
    + \iint_{\tilde{A}_{x,\tilde{n}}} \mathbf{J} \cdot d\mathbf{A}.
\end{equation}
The left-hand side is approximated by the four tangential $\mathbf{H}$ components
on the surrounding dual edges, weighted by the dual edge lengths. Reading from
Fig.~\ref{fig:yee_curl}:
\begin{align}
    \oint_{\partial \tilde{A}_{x,\tilde{n}}} \mathbf{H} \cdot d\mathbf{s}
    &\approx
        + \tilde{l}_{y,\,n+N_x}\, H_{y,\,n+N_x}
        - \tilde{l}_{y,\,n+N_x+1}\, H_{y,\,n+N_x+1} \nonumber\\
    &\quad
        - \tilde{l}_{z,\,n+1}\, H_{z,\,n+1}
        + \tilde{l}_{z,\,n+N_x+1}\, H_{z,\,n+N_x+1},
\end{align}

while the right-hand side involves the normal $\mathbf{D}$ and $\mathbf{J}$
components weighted by the dual face area, expressed in terms of $\mathbf{e}$ via
$\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{D}_\varepsilon\,\mathbf{e}$:
\begin{equation}
    \iint_{\tilde{A}_{x,\tilde{n}}} \frac{\partial \mathbf{D}}{\partial t}
        \cdot d\mathbf{A}
    \approx \tilde{A}_{x,\tilde{n}}\,\varepsilon_n\,
        \frac{\partial E_{x,\tilde{n}}}{\partial t}, \qquad
    \iint_{\tilde{A}_{x,\tilde{n}}} \mathbf{J} \cdot d\mathbf{A}
    \approx \tilde{A}_{x,\tilde{n}}\,J_{x,\tilde{n}}.
\end{equation}
Assembling over all three face orientations and all $N$ dual cells gives the second
Maxwell Grid Equation:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\tilde{\mathbf{C}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_s\,\mathbf{h}
    = \tilde{\mathbf{D}}_A\,\mathbf{D}_\varepsilon\,
        \frac{\partial \mathbf{e}}{\partial t}
    + \tilde{\mathbf{D}}_A\,\mathbf{j}}
    \label{eq:MGE:ampere}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Gauss's law for magnetism $\longrightarrow$ Primal Divergence Equation}

Applying Gauss's law for magnetic fields~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:c} to primal cell $n$,
the net outward magnetic flux through its six faces must vanish:
\begin{equation}
    \oiint_{\partial V_n} \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{A} = 0.
\end{equation}
Each face contributes one normal $\mathbf{B}$ component weighted by its primal face
area, expressed in terms of $\mathbf{h}$ via $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{D}_\mu\,\mathbf{h}$.
Reading the signed contributions from Fig.~\ref{fig:yee_divergence}:

\begin{align}
    \oiint_{\partial V_n} \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{A}
    &\approx
        - A_{x,n}\,\mu_n\, H_{x,n}
        + A_{x,\,n+1}\,\mu_{n+1}\, H_{x,\,n+1} \nonumber\\
    &\quad
        - A_{y,n}\,\mu_n\, H_{y,n}
        + A_{y,\,n+N_x}\,\mu_{n+N_x}\, H_{y,\,n+N_x} \nonumber\\
    &\quad
        - A_{z,n}\,\mu_n\, H_{z,n}
        + A_{z,\,n+N_xN_y}\,\mu_{n+N_xN_y}\, H_{z,\,n+N_xN_y}
    = 0.
\end{align}

Assembling over all $N$ primal cells and separating topology from metric gives the
third Maxwell Grid Equation:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{D}_A\,\mathbf{D}_\mu\,\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{0}}
    \label{eq:MGE:gauss_mag}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Gauss's law for electricity $\longrightarrow$ Dual Divergence Equation}

Applying Gauss's law for electric fields~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:d} to dual cell
$\tilde{n}$, the net outward electric flux through its six faces equals the enclosed
free charge:
\begin{equation}
    \oiint_{\partial \tilde{V}_{\tilde{n}}} \mathbf{D} \cdot d\mathbf{A}
    = \iiint_{\tilde{V}_{\tilde{n}}} \rho \, dV.
\end{equation}
Each dual face contributes one normal $\mathbf{D}$ component weighted by its dual
face area, expressed in terms of $\mathbf{e}$ via
$\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{D}_\varepsilon\,\mathbf{e}$.
Reading from Fig.~\ref{fig:yee_divergence}:
\begin{align}
    \oiint_{\partial \tilde{V}_{\tilde{n}}} \mathbf{D} \cdot d\mathbf{A}
    &\approx
        - \tilde{A}_{x,n}\,\varepsilon_n\, E_{x,n}
        + \tilde{A}_{x,\,n+1}\,\varepsilon_{n+1}\, E_{x,\,n+1} \nonumber\\
    &\quad
        - \tilde{A}_{y,n}\,\varepsilon_n\, E_{y,n}
        + \tilde{A}_{y,\,n+N_x}\,\varepsilon_{n+N_x}\, E_{y,\,n+N_x} \nonumber\\
    &\quad
        - \tilde{A}_{z,n}\,\varepsilon_n\, E_{z,n}
        + \tilde{A}_{z,\,n+N_xN_y}\,\varepsilon_{n+N_xN_y}\,
            E_{z,\,n+N_xN_y},
\end{align}
while the right-hand side is approximated by the total discrete charge $q_n$
enclosed in the dual cell volume $\tilde{V}_{\tilde{n}}$. Assembling over all $N$
dual cells gives the fourth Maxwell Grid Equation:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\tilde{\mathbf{S}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_A\,\mathbf{D}_\varepsilon\,
        \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{q}}
    \label{eq:MGE:gauss_el}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{q}$ is the $N$-vector of discrete free charges enclosed in each dual
cell.

\subsubsection*{Summary}

The four Maxwell Grid Equations~\eqref{eq:MGE:faraday}--\eqref{eq:MGE:gauss_el}
form a complete discrete system that is the exact grid-space counterpart of the
continuous Maxwell equations~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:a}--\eqref{eq:Maxwell:d}:

The separation between topology --- encoded in $\mathbf{C}$, $\tilde{\mathbf{C}}$,
$\mathbf{S}$, $\tilde{\mathbf{S}}$ --- and metric --- encoded in $\mathbf{D}_s$,
$\mathbf{D}_A$, $\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_s$, $\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_A$ --- together with
the material properties encoded in $\mathbf{D}_\varepsilon$, $\mathbf{D}_\mu$,
$\mathbf{D}_\sigma$, is a defining feature of the FIT framework. The topological
operators change only when the grid connectivity changes, while the metric and
material matrices are the only quantities that need to be updated when the grid is
refined or the material distribution is modified, making the method straightforward
to implement and maintain.

\subsection{Divergence conservation}

An important algebraic identity satisfied by the Curl $C$ and the Divergence $S$ pair of operators is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{C} = 0, \qquad
    \tilde{\mathbf{S}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{C}} = 0,
    \label{eq:divcurl}
\end{equation}
which is the discrete analogue of the vector identity
$\mathbf{div}\,\mathbf{curl} \equiv 0$. This property holds exactly at the matrix
level, independently of the mesh geometry. To see why, consider a single primal
cell $n$ and compute the $n$-th row of $\mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{C}$ acting on
$\mathbf{e}$. The primal divergence $\mathbf{S}$ sums the six face fluxes of
$\mathbf{C}\,\mathbf{e}$, i.e.\ the six face circulations of $\mathbf{E}$.
Each primal edge of cell $n$ is shared by exactly two faces of that same cell,
and the two faces induce opposite circulation orientations on their shared edge,
as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:yee_divergence}. Taking the $z$-direction as an
example, the contribution of edge $E_{z,n}$ enters the $x$-face circulation with
a $+1$ and the $y$-face circulation with a $-1$:
\begin{equation}
    (\mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{C}\,\mathbf{e})_n
    = \sum_{\text{faces of } n}
      \underbrace{(\mathbf{C}\,\mathbf{e})_{\text{face}}}_{\text{circulation}}
    = \sum_{\text{edges of } n}
      \underbrace{(+1 - 1)}_{\text{opposite orientations}} E_{\text{edge}}
    = 0
\end{equation}
Since this cancellation occurs for every edge of every primal cell, the full matrix
product vanishes identically:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{0}.
\end{equation}
The same argument applied to the dual grid gives $\tilde{\mathbf{S}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{C}} = \mathbf{0}$,
and since $\tilde{\mathbf{C}} = \mathbf{C}^t$ and
$\tilde{\mathbf{S}} = \mathbf{S}^t$, the two identities are in fact
related by transposition:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{S}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{C}}
    = \mathbf{S}^t\,(\mathbf{C}^t)
    = (\mathbf{C}\,\mathbf{S})^t
    = \mathbf{0} 
\end{equation}

These identities have a direct physical consequence for both Gauss laws. Consider
first the magnetic Gauss law~\eqref{eq:MGE:gauss_mag}. Taking the time derivative
and substituting the Faraday MGE~\eqref{eq:MGE:faraday}:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{D}_A\,\mathbf{D}_\mu\,\mathbf{h}\right)
    = \mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{D}_A\,\mathbf{D}_\mu\,
      \frac{\partial \mathbf{h}}{\partial t}
    = -\mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{C}\,\mathbf{D}_s\,\mathbf{e}
    = \mathbf{0}.
\end{equation}
For the electric Gauss law~\eqref{eq:MGE:gauss_el}, taking the time derivative
and substituting the Amp\`ere MGE~\eqref{eq:MGE:ampere}:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{dt}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{S}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_A\,
      \mathbf{D}_\varepsilon\,\mathbf{e}\right)
    = \tilde{\mathbf{S}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_A\,\mathbf{D}_\varepsilon\,
      \frac{\partial \mathbf{e}}{\partial t}
    = \tilde{\mathbf{S}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_A\,
      \frac{\partial \mathbf{d}}{\partial t}
    = \tilde{\mathbf{S}}\left(
        \tilde{\mathbf{C}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_s\,\mathbf{h}
        - \tilde{\mathbf{D}}_A\,\mathbf{j}
      \right)
    = -\tilde{\mathbf{S}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_A\,\mathbf{j},
\end{equation}

where the last step uses $\tilde{\mathbf{S}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{C}} = \mathbf{0}$.
This is precisely the discrete charge continuity equation: provided that the
source current $\mathbf{j}$ satisfies charge conservation, the electric Gauss
law is also preserved throughout the time evolution if it holds at $t = 0$.

Both Gauss laws~\eqref{eq:MGE:gauss_mag} and~\eqref{eq:MGE:gauss_el} are
therefore \emph{redundant} with respect to the two curl
equations~\eqref{eq:MGE:faraday} and~\eqref{eq:MGE:ampere}, in the sense that
they are automatically satisfied for all $t > 0$ provided that the initial
conditions are divergence-free and the source is charge-conserving. This is a
direct consequence of the Yee staggering introduced in Sec.~\ref{sec:mge_yee},
which ensures that the discrete curl and divergence operators are each other's
adjoint by construction. In the FIT framework, this geometric property is
elevated to an exact algebraic identity at the matrix level, independently of
the mesh geometry and material distribution. The practical implication is
significant: in a time-domain simulation it is sufficient to advance only the
two curl equations~\eqref{eq:MGE:faraday} and~\eqref{eq:MGE:ampere} in time,
and the divergence constraints are guaranteed to be preserved without any
additional enforcement or correction step.
