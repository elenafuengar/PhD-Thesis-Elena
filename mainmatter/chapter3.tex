\section{Beam-coupling impedance computation challenges}
% Brief recap of the state of the art + statement of current solver's limitations
This section reviews the state of the art in wakefield and impedance simulations and lays out the present computation challenges. These
challenges, together with current solvers' limitations, motivate the development of a new, open-source wakefield solver capable of
addressing present and future impedance simulation requirements.

\subsection{State of the art of impedance simulations}

Beam-coupling impedance and wakefields are critical to the design and operation of particle accelerators. As charged particle beams traverse
accelerator structures, interactions with geometric discontinuities and material inhomogeneities generate \gls{em} fields---known as
wakefields---that degrade beam stability, cause beam-induced heating, and limit machine performance \cite{metral_impedance_2020}. The
frequency-domain counterpart of these interactions is the beam-coupling impedance, an intrinsic property of each component used to quantify
wakefield effects. Correctly predicting their impact on beam stability and machine heating requires an accurate, fullwave evaluation of the
beam-coupling impedance of the main accelerator components---covering both long-range narrowband resonances and short-range broadband
sources from resistive wall.

Analytical methods provide accurate impedance predictions for simplified structures such as resistive walls \cite{yokoya_resistive_1993,
migliorati_resistive_2019}, RF cavities \cite{hofmann_analytic_1990, jensen_rf_2014}, and transitions such as tapers or steps
\cite{yokoya_impedance_1990, stupakov_low_2007, palumbo_wake_1995}. For realistic components with complex geometries, however, the full
three-dimensional solution of Maxwell's equations \cite{maxwell_viii_1997} is required.

Maxwell's equations can be solved numerically in either frequency or time domain, using several discretization approaches. Among the most
widely used are the \gls{fdtd} method, the \gls{fem}, and the \gls{fvtd} method, which differ primarily in the formulation of the governing
equations (differential or weak form), the choice of basis functions, and the handling of material interfaces and boundary conditions.

In the frequency domain, for lossless, source-free structures, the problem reduces to an eigenmode formulation, as used in CST Eigenmode,
HFSS, or ACE3P's Omega3P. Time-domain solvers instead offer full-wave impedance characterization by directly computing Maxwell's equations
in integral (see Eqs.~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:a}--\eqref{eq:Maxwell:d}) or differential form---the latter commonly known as the \gls{fdtd} method.
Commercial solvers such as \gls{cst}~\cite{cst_studio_suite} Wakefield, \gls{gdfidl}~\cite{bruns_gdfidl_1997}, and closed-source
\gls{echo4d}~\cite{zagorodnov_echo4d} and PBCI~\cite{Lau:1182975} use the \gls{fit} \cite{weiland_finite_2003}, while ACE3P's T3P employs
the \gls{fem} \cite{candel_ace3p_2010}.

Several open-source 3D solvers implement full-wave time-domain methods, such as Meep \cite{oskooi_meep_2010} and \gls{warpx}
\cite{fedeli_pushing_2022}, both based on the \gls{fdtd} method \cite{yee_numerical_1966}, and \gls{emclaw} \cite{moreno_emclaw_2021}, which
uses a \gls{fvtd} Godunov scheme. However, none of these tools offer the features needed for beam-coupling impedance calculations: native
\gls{cad} geometry support, advanced material modeling, and wakefield-tailored post-processing.

At the time of this thesis, there is therefore no open-source alternative capable of treating arbitrarily shaped 3D accelerator components
and delivering a fullwave, long-range converged impedance result. On top of this gap, the field faces growing challenges in performing such
simulations with the tools currently available.

\subsection{Challenges from future accelerator projects}
% Introduction to \gls{fcc} project and Muon collider project

Despite more than 30 years of Wakefield-solver development, impedance evaluation remains challenging---and these challenges intensify
further for future accelerator projects that push the boundaries of current designs. At CERN, two main projects are under discussion as
successors to the \gls{lhc} and \gls{hl-lhc}, each bringing new beam-coupling impedance computation challenges.

\subsubsection*{FCC impedance challenges}
% Cite \gls{fcc} papers by Chiara and Carlo
CERN's leading option is the \gls{fcc}, a proposed \SI{91}{\kilo\meter} circular collider to be built in a much larger tunnel beneath the
Geneva region, succeeding the \gls{lhc}. It is planned in two stages: \gls{fcc-ee}, an electron-positron collider for precision Higgs,
electroweak, and top-quark physics starting in the 2040s, followed by \gls{fcc}-hh, a proton-proton collider reaching energies around
\SI{100}{\tera\electronvolt} to search directly for physics beyond the Standard Model.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Short bunches.} One of the key challenges for future high-energy colliders such as the \gls{fcc} lies in its design parameters,
already demanding in the \gls{fcc-ee} regime. Simulating \gls{fcc-ee} accelerator components is computationally heavy because of the short
design bunch length of 4 to \SI{12}{\milli\meter}. As explained in Chapter 2, bunch length sets the frequency range excited by the passing
bunch: the shorter the bunch, the wider the excited spectrum, following Fourier Transform principles. Resolving this short bunch on the
simulation mesh (as a rule of thumb, at least 20 cells per wavelength) leaves longitudinal mesh cells below a millimeter, in components that
can be meters long.

\item \textbf{Slowly decaying wakefields.} Accelerator components are also often built from highly conductive materials (e.g.\ copper) to
reduce their impedance. This slows wakefield decay, pushing the required simulation length---the wakelength---into the kilometer range to
fully resolve impedance resonance amplitudes.

\item \textbf{Propagating fields and element cross-talk.} The design beam pipe aperture is large relative to the bunch length, so some
electromagnetic modes excited by the passing bunch no longer stay resonant in the device but propagate through the beam pipe above its
cutoff frequency. This introduces a further challenge: possible cross-talk between accelerator components, which requires full control of
the solver's open-boundary conditions to simulate and understand.

\item \textbf{High beam current and machine size.} The \gls{fcc-ee}'s high beam current---11200 bunches at \num{2.17e11} protons per bunch for the
Z-mode, at the time of writing---combined with its \SI{91}{\kilo\meter} circumference, raises the accuracy demands on beam-coupling
impedance calculations given the tight constraints on beam stability and beam-induced heating.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Muon Collider impedance challenges}
% Cite Elias and Erik's papers

A more long-term, first-of-its-kind option is the Muon Collider, part of the \gls{imcc}. It is a design study for a future collider that
would smash muons and anti-muons together at multi-TeV energies. Because muons are far heavier than electrons, they lose much less energy to
synchrotron radiation, making a compact, high-energy lepton collider possible---but their short (\SI{2.2}{\micro\second}) lifetime demands
extremely fast production, cooling, and acceleration.

The Muon Collider adds further challenges that current commercial and non-commercial solvers cannot handle directly.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Impedance in matter.} During ionization cooling, muons propagate inside matter rather than vacuum, requiring an extension of
the analytical formalism. Based on a field-matching technique [CITE], this formalism has recently been extended to material-filled
geometries, predicting impedance resonances with a predictable periodicity as the beam traverses a dielectric absorber. These resonances are
attenuated once conductive losses are included, but remain a significant contribution compared to the vacuum-only case [CITE]. This extended
formalism still needs numerical validation. However, current commercial solvers do not support beam currents traversing materials other than
vacuum. One relevant case is the Muon Collider's RF cavities in the cooling channel: copper cavities with beryllium windows at the entrance
and exit, designed to perform 6D ionization cooling and reduce muon emittance by several orders of magnitude, while the RF cavities restore
the longitudinal momentum lost in the absorbers [CITE].

\item \textbf{Non-ultra-relativistic beams.} The ionization cooling regime is challenging both because of the absorbers and because the
beam cannot be treated as ultra-relativistic: the muon relativistic factor $\beta$ ranges from 0.88 to 0.92, so the simulated impedance
includes a \gls{sc} contribution that must be separated from the cavity-driven impedance. Simulating non-ultra-relativistic beams is
difficult, and only advanced solvers such as \gls{cst} Wakefield can currently do so [CITE]---yet isolating the \gls{sc} contribution
remains a challenge no existing \gls{em} solver addresses.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Challenges in present particle accelerators}
% Cite Chiaras paper and me & Leo's IPAC paper and me & Carlo's HB paper

Beam-coupling impedance challenges are not limited to future accelerators. As described in Chapter 2, present high-energy, high-intensity
accelerators already suffer impedance-driven component damage that can cause machine downtime and instrumentation failure, as seen in the
\gls{lhc} and its injector chain. As beam current and intensity increase, more impedance-driven problems arise, making the ability to understand,
model, and predict impedance-driven element failure essential for operating accelerators in the coming HL-\gls{lhc} era.

One recent example is the SPS BWS failure, where four beam-wire-scanner measurement wires sublimated overnight under high-intensity
operation. The issue was addressed urgently with a new design, based on Wakefield simulations, aimed at reducing beam-induced heating on the
wire.

Another is the \gls{lhc} Warm Vacuum interconnecting modules. These include thin, highly conductive fingers that maintain electromagnetic
continuity between the beampipe and the transition tube, shielding the corrugated bellow behind them. The bellows accommodate thermal
expansion and compression, but their unshielded contribution to impedance would be too large to leave untreated. However, these thin fingers
can degrade over time, drifting from their design configuration and increasing both impedance and beam-induced heating. This heating can be
non-uniform across the device: if localized by the resonant mode composition, it can damage or---as with the \gls{lhc} Warm Vacuum
modules---melt and plasticize components, an effect known as the impedance loop, where higher power loss causes thermomechanical stress that
further changes (and typically worsens) the device's impedance.

\subsection{Key features needed in a next-generation Wakefield solver}

Fullwave impedance simulations of 3D accelerator components with arbitrary geometry and conductive or dispersive materials have so far only
been possible with black-box commercial solvers. Despite 30 years of development and solid benchmarking, their lack of transparency makes
them rigid, and they represent a single point of failure for complex impedance evaluations. The impedance field keeps evolving, demanding a
solver that can be extended to meet newly arising challenges. Figure \ref{fig:EMfeatures} gathers the key features identified for our
next-generation Wakefield solver, and the level of maturity on different EM solver options.

\begin{figure}[htb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures_ch3/fig_wakefield_features.pdf}
\caption{Table showing the key features needed for a complete Wakefield solver and their availability across different commercial and open-source EM solver options}
\label{fig:EMfeatures}
\end{figure}

Starting from left to right, we believe that a modern solver needs to be open-source and fully transparent, allowing continuous improvement
and extension. Among the most promising open-source options, \gls{warpx}~\cite{fedeli_pushing_2022} --- a mature and powerful,
GPU-accelerated particle-in-cell code with an active developer community and a modern Python interface, widely used across the plasma and
beam-physics community --- and \gls{emclaw}~\cite{moreno_emclaw_2021} --- a compact FVTD solver whose unsplit Godunov scheme avoids flux
limiters by interpolating only the field components that remain continuous across material interfaces, making it both simpler and
competitive in speed with \gls{fdtd} --- still lack, or only partially support, some of the fundamental features needed for 3D accelerator
wakefield simulations: \gls{cad} geometry import, material losses, beam excitation injection, and long-range wakefield computation.

\gls{echo4d}~\cite{zagorodnov_echo4d} is a closed-source code well regarded in the impedance community for its accuracy, with solid
\gls{cad} import from \gls{stl} geometry. Thanks to its co-moving window and multithreading, it is computationally efficient even without
GPU acceleration, though it does not include material losses and its co-moving window approach is not intended for long-range wakefields.

Among the commercial solvers, \gls{cst}~\cite{cst_studio_suite} Wakefield solver --- the most established and extensively benchmarked
full-wave tool in the accelerator community, and CERN's go-to choice --- and \gls{gdfidl}~\cite{bruns_gdfidl_1997} --- a mature, efficient
\gls{fdtd} solver --- both lack GPU acceleration and a transparent Python interface; \gls{gdfidl} further forces a uniform mesh and also
uses the co-moving window, limiting it to short-range wakefields. Of all options, \gls{cst} remains the most complete tool and is the
reference for beam-coupling impedance simulations at CERN.

\clearpage
\subsubsection{The Wakis project pillars}

\gls{wakis}~\cite{de_la_fuente_garcia_wakis_2025} began as a post-processing module attached to WarpX's \gls{fdtd} solver, computing wake
potential and impedance from WarpX's pre-computed fields~\cite{de_la_fuente_generalized_2023}. Since WarpX lacked material tensors, modeled
the beam current only via \gls{cad}-free PIC current deposition of millions of macroparticles---computationally costly---and its open
boundaries (an adjusted \gls{pml}~\cite{berenger_perfectly_1994}) showed important limitations when the beam was injected or modes were
excited above cutoff~\cite{de_la_fuente_generalized_2023}, the idea of a new solver built from scratch took shape, giving rise to this PhD
topic.

Together with the identified key features, four design principles guided this new solver:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Open-source approach.} In line with CERN's open-source mission and its goal of building in-house electromagnetic-simulation expertise, \gls{wakis} is designed to be transparent and accessible to the wider accelerator community. An open, collaborative framework lets impedance-evaluation expertise from CERN and other institutions be pooled, and lets the tool grow beyond a single developer or institution. It also arrives as AI-assisted code development becomes mainstream: LLMs can help physicists and engineers with limited coding experience read and extend open-source code, build digital twins of accelerator components, and automate simulation and design-iteration steps---opportunities closed-source tools cannot offer.

\item \textbf{Trusted numerical method.} The numerical method underlying \gls{wakis} is the \gls{fit}, the formalism trusted by the commercial solvers proven most accurate for impedance evaluations, and one with no existing open-source implementation. \gls{fit} discretizes Maxwell's equations in integral form into large sparse matrix operators, which---unlike the loop-based \gls{fdtd}---are contiguous in memory and therefore \gls{gpu}-friendly. Its formulation also naturally accommodates embedded boundaries between material regions through rank-2 material tensors, and reduces the addition of the passing beam current to a time-dependent external source term.

\item \textbf{Modularity and maintainability.} \gls{wakis} is written in Python, chosen as an entry-level, widely adopted language that lowers the barrier for new contributors and users alike. The code follows modern software-engineering practices---modular design, version control, continuous integration and testing, and documented, interactive examples---to keep the solver maintainable and extendable well beyond this thesis. Chapter 4 details this design and the resulting code-quality infrastructure.

\item \textbf{Memory and computation performant.} Extending \gls{wakis} to new physics---e.g.\ impedance in matter or component cross-talk---demands an extremely fine, accurate mesh to capture geometry detail, and fast execution to complete simulations and iterate on component design in reasonable time. Computational speed and memory management were therefore a central focus of this thesis, in particular making the implementation \gls{gpu} compatible---a first for Wakefield solvers in this field.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Finite Integration Technique}
\label{sec:mge_final}

The beauty of numerical methods lies in their ability to turn complex equations into computer-digestible operators that can be solved
efficiently. In this section, we introduce the \gls{fit}, the numerical method chosen to build this thesis' Wakefield solver, \gls{wakis}.

The goal of a Wakefield solver is to solve Maxwell's equations in a box-shaped simulation domain that includes arbitrarily 3D-shaped
material regions, under certain boundary conditions, and under the excitation of a passing beam source. To achieve this, we start from the
most general approach, the integral form of Maxwell's equations.

Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^3$ denote the computational domain with boundary $\partial\Omega$ and outward unit normal $\mathbf{n}$. In
\gls{fit}, the \gls{em} field evolution is formulated starting from the general integral form of Maxwell equations, written for an arbitrary
oriented surface $A$ with line boundary $\partial A$ and an arbitrary volume $V$ with surface boundary $\partial V$:

\begin{align}
\oint_{\partial A} \mathbf{E}\cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{s} &= -\iint_{A}\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}\cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{A} \label{eq:Maxwell:a}\\[6pt]
\oint_{\partial A} \mathbf{H}\cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{s} &= \iint_{A}\left(\frac{\partial \mathbf{D}}{\partial t} + \mathbf{J}\right)\cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{A} \label{eq:Maxwell:b}\\[6pt]
\oiint_{\partial V} \mathbf{B}\cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{A} &= 0 \label{eq:Maxwell:c}\\[6pt]
\oiint_{\partial V} \mathbf{D}\cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{A} &= \iiint_{V}\rho\, \mathrm{d}V \label{eq:Maxwell:d}
\end{align}

Equations~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:a}--\eqref{eq:Maxwell:d} represent the evolution of the electric field $\mathbf{E}$, magnetic flux density
$\mathbf{B}$, electric displacement field $\mathbf{D}$, and magnetic field intensity $\mathbf{H}$, in the presence of a current density
$\mathbf{J}$ and charge density $\rho$. Taken together, they govern all classical electromagnetic phenomena and form the foundation of the
numerical method developed in this work:

\begin{mydescription}
\item[Faraday's law~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:a}:] the circulation of the electric
field $\mathbf{E}$ around the closed boundary $\partial A$ of any oriented surface $A$ equals the negative rate of change of the magnetic
flux through that surface. This relation captures the fundamental coupling between time-varying magnetic fields and induced electric fields.

\item[Amp\`ere--Maxwell law~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:b}:] the circulation of the
magnetic field intensity $\mathbf{H}$ around $\partial A$ is driven by two contributions --- the conduction current density $\mathbf{J}$ and
the displacement current $\partial \mathbf{D}/\partial t$, the latter introduced by Maxwell to ensure consistency with charge conservation.
These source terms enable the propagation of electromagnetic waves even in the absence of free charges.

\item[Gauss's law for magnetic fields~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:c}:] the net magnetic
flux through any closed surface $\partial V$ is identically zero, expressing the absence of magnetic monopoles. This constraint is satisfied
by construction in the discrete setting when the initial conditions are divergence-free.

\item[Gauss's law for electric fields~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:d}:] the total electric
flux through a closed surface $\partial V$ equals the net free charge enclosed in the volume $V$. Similarly to Eq.~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:c},
this relation is automatically preserved at the discrete level by the consistency of the primal--dual grid construction.
\end{mydescription}

The constitutive relations close the system by linking the field intensities $(\mathbf{E},\mathbf{H})$ to the flux densities
$(\mathbf{D},\mathbf{B})$. In the most general (possibly anisotropic) case,
\begin{align}
\mathbf{D} &= \underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}\,\mathbf{E}, \qquad
\mathbf{B} = \underline{\underline{\mu}}\,\mathbf{H}, \qquad
\mathbf{J} = \underline{\underline{\sigma}}\,\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{src}},
\label{eq:constitutive_continuum}
\end{align}

The constitutive relations in Eq.~\eqref{eq:constitutive_continuum} relate these fields through the material properties: permittivity
$\varepsilon$, permeability $\mu$, and conductivity $\sigma$. They are represented as $\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}$,
$\underline{\underline{\mu}}$ and $\underline{\underline{\sigma}}$, which are (in general space-dependent) second-order tensors, and
$\mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{src}}$ denotes impressed sources such as the particle beam current. Throughout this work,
$\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}$, $\underline{\underline{\mu}}$ and $\underline{\underline{\sigma}}$ are treated as
frequency-independent, non-dispersive tensors --- an approximation justified by the weakly dispersive behaviour of the conductive and
dielectric materials typically found in accelerator components. Accounting for frequency or time dependence would instead require replacing
the products in Eq.~\eqref{eq:constitutive_continuum} by convolutions in time.

Applying \gls{fit}, we transform these continuous integrals into discrete matricial operators, arriving at the so-called \glspl{mge}. Under
this thesis' nomenclature, they read:

\begin{align}
\mathbf{C}\,\mathbf{D}_s\,\mathbf{e}&= -\mathbf{D}_A\,\frac{\partial \mathbf{b}}{\partial t} \label{eq:MGE:faraday}\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}\,\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_s\,\mathbf{h} &= \widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_A\left(\frac{\partial \mathbf{d}}{\partial t} + \mathbf{j}\right) \label{eq:MGE:ampere} \\
\mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{D}_A\, \mathbf{b} &= 0 \label{eq:MGE:gauss_mag} \\
\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}\,\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_A\,\left( \frac{\partial \mathbf{d}}{\partial t} +\mathbf{j} \right) & = 0 \label{eq:MGE:gauss_el}
\end{align}

Where: $\mathbf{e}$ and $\mathbf{h}$ are the vectors of discrete electric and magnetic field \glspl{dof}, stored on the edges of the primal
and dual grids introduced in Sec.~\ref{sec:mge_yee}. $\mathbf{C}$ and $\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}$ are the discrete curl operators, while
$\mathbf{S}$ and $\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}$ are the discrete divergence operators, both defined in Sec.~\ref{sec:mge_operators}.
$\mathbf{D}_s$, $\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_s$, $\mathbf{D}_A$ and $\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_A$ are diagonal matrices of primal and dual edge
lengths and face areas, encoding the grid metric. $\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{d}$ are the discrete magnetic and electric flux density vectors,
and $\mathbf{j}$ the discrete total current density, related to $\mathbf{e}$ and $\mathbf{h}$ through the discrete material matrices
$\mathbf{M}_\varepsilon$, $\mathbf{M}_\mu$ and $\mathbf{M}_\sigma$ in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:MGE:constit_b}--\eqref{eq:MGE:constit_j}. These
matrices are built from the continuum tensors $\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}$, $\underline{\underline{\mu}}$ and
$\underline{\underline{\sigma}}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:constitutive_continuum}, as detailed in Sec.~\ref{sec:em_materials}. Finally,
$\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{src}}$ is the discrete representation of the impressed source current $\mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{src}}$, which for a
wakefield solver represents the passing beam excitation. Finally, the flux densities $(d,b)$ and current $j$ can be obtained via the discrete constitutive (material) relations closing the system, in direct analogy with Eq.~\eqref{eq:constitutive_continuum}:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{b} &= \mathbf{M}_{\mu}\,\mathbf{h}, \label{eq:MGE:constit_b}\\
\mathbf{d} &= \mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\,\mathbf{e}, \label{eq:MGE:constit_d}\\
\mathbf{j} &= \mathbf{M}_{\sigma}\,\mathbf{e} + \mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{src}}. \label{eq:MGE:constit_j}
\end{align}


The following sections give the recipe to build each ingredient of the MGE.

\subsection{The computational grid $G$}
\label{sec:mge_integral}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures_ch3/fig_computational_grid.pdf}
\caption{Schematic of the simulation domain discretization. The domain is
considered as a rectangular box subdivided into individual computational cells composing the Cartesian grid. Each computational cell $n$ is
ordered using the lexicographic indexing convention.}
\label{fig:comp_grid}
\end{figure}

The path of turning Maxwell's equations into the \glspl{mge} starts with a first approximation, the discretization of the simulation
computational domain $\Omega$, in our case considered an arbitrarily shaped box. In order to numerically solve the presented set of
equations, the computational domain $\Omega$ is discretized into a set number of hexahedral computational cells in each coordinate direction
$x$, $y$, $z$, forming a computational grid $\mathbf{G}$ of $\{N_x \times N_y
\times N_z\}$ cells --- often also referred to as \textit{simulation mesh}. The grid $G$ is defined in the orthogonal coordinate system $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}(x,y,z)$ as:

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathbf{G} = \{ (x_i,\, y_j,\, z_k)\quad
x_0 < x_i < x_{N_x},\ i = 1,\ldots,N_x-1\quad \\ y_0 < y_j < y_{N_y},\ j = 1,\ldots,N_y-1\quad \\ z_0 < z_k < z_{N_z},\ k = 1,\ldots,N_z-1
\}
\end{split}
\label{eq:grid}
\end{equation}

From this spatial construction of the grid, all grid nodes $n$ (a total of $N = N_x N_y N_z$) of each computational cell in $\mathbf{G}$ are
numbered linearly following the lexicographic indexing convention (see \autoref{def:Lexicographic_indexing}):

\begin{equation}
n = iI+ jJ + kK,
\qquad n = 0,\ldots,N - 1.
\end{equation}

The quantities $I,\ J,\ K$ can be chosen to follow different ordering conventions. In the original \gls{fit} formulation
\cite{weiland_wake_1992}, the choice $I = 1,\ J = N_x,\ K = N_xN_y$ sets a Fortran-order scheme, which is also followed in this thesis.

\begin{definition}{Lexicographic indexing}{Lexicographic_indexing}
A bijective mapping from multi-dimensional grid indices $(i, j, k)$ to a single linear index $n \in [0,\, N_x N_y N_z)$. Two standard
conventions exist:
\textbf{Fortran-order} (column-major), where the first index varies fastest,
$n = i + N_x j + N_x N_y k$, and \textbf{C-order} (row-major), where the last index varies fastest, $n = k + N_z j + N_z N_y i$. The choice
of ordering convention directly affects the sparsity pattern of the \gls{fit} matrices and has implications for memory access efficiency and
solver performance.
\end{definition}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:comp_grid}, each computational cell $n$ is identified by an index triplet $\{i,\ j,\ k\}$, where $i$ represents
the index along the $x$-direction, $j$ along the $y$-direction, and $k$ along the $z$-direction. The resulting ordered set of indices allows
the field information to be stored in arrays and the matrix operators that numerically replace the continuous integral operators to be
constructed.

\subsection{The computational cell $n$}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures_ch3/fig_computational_cell.pdf}
\caption{Schematic of the quantities allocation of each computational cell $n$.}
\label{fig:comp_cell}
\end{figure}

The field distribution in the volume of interest is replaced by discrete field values on each computational grid cell $n$. Rather than
assigning all six field components to grid nodes (which causes problems at material interfaces where some components are discontinuous), the
components of $\mathbf{E}$ are placed at the midpoints of cell edges $L= l_x, \ l_y, \ l_z$, and the components of the magnetic field
$\mathbf{H}$ pierce the center of each cell face $A = A_x, \ A_y, \ A_z$. Figure~\ref{fig:comp_cell} shows a schematic of the base
computational cell $n$, together with the field component locations and their indexation.

\subsubsection*{Cell lengths and areas}

Each computational cell $n$, identified by the index triplet $(i,j,k)$, is fully described by its three edge lengths and three face areas.
The edge lengths are the distances between adjacent grid nodes along each coordinate direction:
\begin{equation}
l_{x,n} = x_{i+1} - x_i, \qquad l_{y,n} = y_{j+1} - y_j, \qquad l_{z,n} = z_{k+1} - z_k.
\label{eq:edge_lengths}
\end{equation}
The face area perpendicular to each coordinate axis is the product of the two transverse edge lengths:
\begin{equation}
A_{x,n} = l_{y,n}\,l_{z,n}, \qquad A_{y,n} = l_{z,n}\,l_{x,n}, \qquad A_{z,n} = l_{x,n}\,l_{y,n}.
\label{eq:face_areas}
\end{equation}
These six scalar quantities per cell carry all the metric information needed to approximate the line and surface integrals in Maxwell's
equations. They are assembled into two $\{3N \times 3N\}$ diagonal matrices, where $N = N_x N_y N_z$ is the total number of cells and the
factor of three accounts for the three spatial components of each field quantity:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{D}_s = \mathrm{Diag}
\!\left(l_{x,1},\ldots,l_{x,N},\;
l_{y,1},\ldots,l_{y,N},\; l_{z,1},\ldots,l_{z,N}\right),
\label{eq:Ds}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{D}_A = \mathrm{Diag}
\!\left(A_{x,1},\ldots,A_{x,N},\;
A_{y,1},\ldots,A_{y,N},\; A_{z,1},\ldots,A_{z,N}\right).
\label{eq:Da}
\end{equation}
$\mathbf{D}_s$ will weight the line integrals of $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ along cell edges, while $\mathbf{D}_A$ will weight the flux
integrals of $\mathbf{B}$ and $\mathbf{D}$ through cell faces. Both matrices appear explicitly in the \glspl{mge} derived in
Sec.~\ref{sec:mge_final}.

\subsubsection*{Field state vectors}

Each computational cell allocates the field state vector components. In the \gls{fit} formalism, they are assembled into global $3N$-vectors
following the lexicographic ordering introduced in Sec.~\ref{sec:mge_integral}:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{e} &= (E_{x,1},\ldots,E_{x,N},\;
E_{y,1},\ldots,E_{y,N},\; E_{z,1},\ldots,E_{z,N})^t, \\[4pt]
\mathbf{h} &= (H_{x,1},\ldots,H_{x,N},\;
H_{y,1},\ldots,H_{y,N},\; H_{z,1},\ldots,H_{z,N})^t.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:field_vectors}
\end{equation}
These two vectors fully describe the electromagnetic state of the system at any given time. All spatial operations in the \gls{fit}
framework --- curl, divergence, and their dual counterparts --- are expressed as matrix--vector products acting on $\mathbf{e}$ and
$\mathbf{h}$.

\begin{definition}{A closer look at \glspl{dof} indexation}{dof indexation}
Notice the field indexes shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:comp_cell}. When moving in the x direction, the lexicographic index $n$ increases by 1.
Therefore the field components $E_{z, n+1}$ and $E_{y, n+1}$ would already belong to the next computational cell $(n+1)$. Note the described
lengths $l_{x,n^*}, \ l_{y,n^*}, \ l_{z,n^*}$, denoted with a star in the figure. The indexes would be correct if we use a uniformly spaced
grid in all three dimensions. However, if different edge lengths per dimension are included in the numerical method, the right indexing for
each length would be $l_{x,n+N_xN_y}, \ l_{y,n+N_xN_y}$ since they would belong to the next cell in the $z$-direction ($n+N_xN_y$), and
$l_{z,n+N_x}$, since this edge length would belong to the next cell in the $y$-direction ($n+N_x$).
\end{definition}

\subsection{Yee cell staggering and the Dual Grid $\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}$}
\label{sec:mge_yee}

\clearpage
\begin{figure}[htb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures_ch3/fig_yee_grid.pdf}
\caption{Pair of Yee-staggered primal and dual cells, showing the \gls{dof} allocation}
\label{fig:yee_cell}
\end{figure}

The reader may have been intrigued by the placement shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:comp_cell} of the magnetic field, located on the faces of the
computational cell on the Grid $G$. Indeed, the allocation of electromagnetic field components in the cell discretization is not arbitrary.
Introduced by Yee~\cite{yee_numerical_1966} in 1966, the staggered-cell arrangement assigns electric and magnetic \glspl{dof} to
complementary geometric objects -- edges and faces -- of two interlocking grids offset by half a cell in each coordinate direction. This
spatial staggering is the key ingredient that confers the scheme its numerical stability, second-order accuracy, and the ability to satisfy
the divergence constraints of Maxwell's equations by construction, without any additional enforcement. In \gls{fit}, this idea is elevated
to a systematic algebraic framework by deriving the discrete operators directly from the integral form of Maxwell's equations on the
primal--dual cell complex, as illustrated in the schematic in Figure~\ref{fig:yee_cell}.

Yee's special field allocation generates a second, \textit{dual grid} $\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}$, in which the magnetic field components
$\mathbf{H}$ are placed where the electric field components $\mathbf{E}$ reside in the \textit{primal grid} $\mathbf{G}$, and vice versa:
the $\mathbf{H}$ components pierce the centres of the primal faces, which coincide with the edges of the dual cells, while the $\mathbf{E}$
components lie on the primal edges, which coincide with the face centres of the dual cells. For a structured grid formulation\footnote{This thesis focuses on structured grids due to their implementation efficiency and stability. However it is possible to extend the FIT formulation to non-structured grids, by taking the baricenter of each primal cell $n$ as boundary vertices for
definition of the dual grid cells of $\tilde{G}$. The FIT formalism will work provided there is a one-to-one
relation between the cell edges of G cutting through the cell surfaces
of ˜G and vice versa},  dual grid $\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}$ is
similarly defined by:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\mathbf{G}} = \{ (\tilde{x}_i,\, \tilde{y}_j,\, \tilde{z}_k)\quad
\tilde{x}_0 < \tilde{x}_i < \tilde{x}_{N_x},\ i = 1,\ldots,N_x-1\quad \\
\tilde{y}_0 < \tilde{y}_j < \tilde{y}_{N_y},\ j = 1,\ldots,N_y-1\quad \\
\tilde{z}_0 < \tilde{z}_k < \tilde{z}_{N_z},\ k = 1,\ldots,N_z-1\}
\end{equation}
with the $\tilde{n}$ dual node coordinates located at the midpoints of the primal cell edges:
\begin{align}
\tilde{x}_i &= \frac{x_{i+1}+x_i}{2}, \quad 1 \le i \le N_x-1,
\qquad \tilde{x}_I = 0, \\
\tilde{y}_j &= \frac{y_{j+1}+y_j}{2}, \quad 1 \le j \le N_y-1,
\qquad \tilde{y}_J = 0, \\
\tilde{z}_k &= \frac{z_{k+1}+z_k}{2}, \quad 1 \le k \le N_z-1,
\qquad \tilde{z}_K = 0.
\end{align}
To preserve the operator matricial shape, the dual grid $\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}$ has the same number of $\tilde n$ nodes $N=N_xN_yN_z$ as
the primal grid $\mathbf{G}$. Notice how, due to the staggering, the last dual node coordinate must be set to zero. The dual grid has one
fewer interior node than the primal grid in each direction: while the primal grid has $I$ nodes in $x$, the dual grid only has $I-1$
midpoints. The boundary node $\tilde{x}_I$ therefore has no corresponding primal interval to bisect, and is set to zero to indicate that it
lies outside the computational domain. In this case, the selected boundary condition (see Sec.~\ref{sec:boundary_conditions}) takes over the
role of closing the outermost dual cell.

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:yee_cell}, each dual cell $\tilde{n}$ is therefore centered on a primal node, and its geometry is described by
dual edge lengths and dual face areas defined in exact analogy with the primal quantities. The dual edge lengths connecting adjacent dual
nodes are:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{l}_{x,n} = \tilde{x}_{i+1} - \tilde{x}_i, \qquad
\tilde{l}_{y,n} = \tilde{y}_{j+1} - \tilde{y}_j, \qquad
\tilde{l}_{z,n} = \tilde{z}_{k+1} - \tilde{z}_k,
\end{equation}
and the dual face areas, perpendicular to each coordinate axis, are:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{A}_{x,n} = \tilde{l}_{y,n}\,\tilde{l}_{z,n}, \qquad
\tilde{A}_{y,n} = \tilde{l}_{z,n}\,\tilde{l}_{x,n}, \qquad
\tilde{A}_{z,n} = \tilde{l}_{x,n}\,\tilde{l}_{y,n}.
\end{equation}

Analogously to Eqs.~\eqref{eq:Ds} and \eqref{eq:Da}, the dual edge lengths and face areas are assembled into the corresponding dual diagonal
matrices $\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_s$ and $\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_A$ of size $\{3N\times3N\}$:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_s = \mathrm{Diag}
\!\left(\tilde{l}_{x,1},\ldots,\tilde{l}_{x,N},\;
\tilde{l}_{y,1},\ldots,\tilde{l}_{y,N},\;
\tilde{l}_{z,1},\ldots,\tilde{l}_{z,N}\right),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_A = \mathrm{Diag}
\!\left(\tilde{A}_{x,1},\ldots,\tilde{A}_{x,N},\;
\tilde{A}_{y,1},\ldots,\tilde{A}_{y,N},\;
\tilde{A}_{z,1},\ldots,\tilde{A}_{z,N}\right).
\end{equation}
The four matrices $\mathbf{D}_s$, $\mathbf{D}_A$, $\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_s$, $\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_A$ together carry all the geometric
information of the primal--dual grid pair, and are therefore completely defined by the simulation mesh choice.

\subsection{Curl $\mathbf{C}$ and divergence $\mathbf{S}$ operators }
\label{sec:mge_operators}

One of the key advantages of the \gls{fit} formulation is that it discretizes the integral form of Maxwell's equations (see Eqs.
~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:a}--\eqref{eq:Maxwell:d}) directly into a set of sparse matrix operators whose entries take only the values $+1$, $-1$,
or $0$, independent of the simulation mesh. Rather than implementing the field updates as nested loops over grid indices --- as is typical
in conventional \gls{fdtd} implementations --- the entire spatial discretization is encoded in the topology matrices $\mathbf{C}$,
$\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}$, $\mathbf{S}$, and $\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}$, which act directly on vectors of field \glspl{dof}. This
representation stays close to the underlying physics: each matrix row corresponds to a concrete geometric operation, either a circulation
integral around a cell edge loop or a flux integral over a cell face, in direct correspondence with the integral laws from which it is
derived. At the same time, expressing the spatial operators as sparse matrix--vector products exposes the full potential for computational
efficiency, enabling vectorized and cache-friendly evaluation on modern hardware without any modification to the mathematical structure of
the equations.

\clearpage

\subsubsection*{Curl operator}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures_ch3/fig_curl.pdf}
\caption{Schematic of the curl operation on a pair of Yee primal and dual cells,
showing the field quantities involved.}
\label{fig:yee_curl}
\end{figure}

The discrete curl operator is constructed by applying Faraday's law in integral form to every face of each primal cell. Following the
approach of Weiland~\cite{weiland_finite_2003}, adapted to this thesis' $(x,y,z)$ nomenclature, a first-order integration formula is used to
approximate the line integral on the left-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:a} along the boundary of an elementary cell face $\partial A$. Consider the
$z$-directed face $A_{z,n}$ of primal cell $n$: only the two tangential components $E_x$ and $E_y$ contribute to the circulation, and the
boundary integral reduces to

\begin{equation}
\oint_{\partial A_{z,n}} \mathbf{E}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s} = l_{x,n}\,E_{x,n} + l_{y,n}\,E_{y,n+1} - l_{x,n}\,E_{x,n+N_x} - l_{y,n}\,E_{y,n}
+ O(l_{x,n}^2) + O(l_{y,n}^2),
\label{eq:curl_lhs}
\end{equation}

where the edge lengths $l_{x,n}$, $l_{y,n}$ are defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:edge_lengths}. Applying the same first-order approximation to the
right-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:a}, the surface integral of the magnetic flux density $\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t$ over the same face $A_{z,n}$ reduces to

\begin{equation}
\iint_{A_{z,n}} \frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf{A} = A_{z,n}\,\frac{\partial B_{z,n}}{\partial t}
+ O\!\left((l_{x,n}\,l_{y,n})^2\right).
\label{eq:curl_rhs}
\end{equation}

Equating Eqs.~\eqref{eq:curl_lhs} and~\eqref{eq:curl_rhs} yields a discrete replacement for Faraday's law on the $z$-directed face of every
cell in the grid. In order to describe these equations for all surfaces in the computational domain, the discretization matrix $C$, the primal curl operator, is introduced. Independent from the lengths and areas (as they're already encoded in their respective diagonal matrices), $C$ is composed only of bands of +1, -1, taking 0 elsewhere. 

An example of the circulation is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:yee_curl}, applied on the z-oriented faces of the dual and primal cell pair.  Focusing only on the primal cell and collecting the contributions with their correct signs gives the $z$-component of the primal curl, which would correspond to one row of the C matrix:

\begin{equation}
(\mathbf{C}\,\mathbf{e})_{z,n} = E_{x,n} - E_{x,n+N_x} - E_{y,n} + E_{y,n+1},
\end{equation}

Notice the field index offsets $I=1$ and $J=N_x$ corresponding to the linear lexico-graphic ordering between the field components that participate in the operation. This reveals the correct placement of the +1 and -1 bands. Repeating this
construction for all three face orientations and all $N$ cells, three elementary difference matrices $\mathbf{P}_x$,
$\mathbf{P}_y$, $\mathbf{P}_z$ can be obtained, each encoding the $\pm1$ connectivity pattern between adjacent nodes along one coordinate direction:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P}_x := (\mathbf{P}_x)_{m,p} = \begin{cases} -1, & p = m \\ +1, & p = m+1 \\ 0, & \text{else} \end{cases} \quad \\
\mathbf{P}_y := (\mathbf{P}_y)_{m,p} = \begin{cases} -1, & p = m \\ +1, & p = m+N_x \\ 0, & \text{else} \end{cases} \quad \\ 
\mathbf{P}_z := (\mathbf{P}_z)_{m,p} = \begin{cases} -1, & p = m \\ +1, & p = m+N_xN_y \\ 0, & \text{else} \end{cases}
\label{eq:Pmatrices}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation*}
m,p = 1,\ldots,N
\end{equation*}

Using the matrices Px, Pz, Py as building blocks, the final sparcity of the curl matrix is obtained:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{C} =
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & -\mathbf{P}_z & \mathbf{P}_y \\
\mathbf{P}_z &  0          & -\mathbf{P}_x \\
-\mathbf{P}_y & \mathbf{P}_x & 0
\end{pmatrix}
\label{eq:C_matrix}
\end{equation}

The dual curl operator $\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}$ is constructed in exactly the same way, but now applied to the faces of the dual cells. Its
role is to compute the circulation of $\mathbf{H}$ around each dual face, as required by Amp\`ere's law.  Fig.~\ref{fig:yee_curl} illustrates the curl operation on H quantities in the z oriented dual face. To give an additional example, we analyze the curl operation applied to and x-oriented dual face. As expected from the matrix sparcity, it should involve only the $H_y$ and $H_z$
components on the surrounding $y$- and $z$-oriented edges:
\begin{equation}
(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}\,\mathbf{h})_{x,\tilde{n}} = -H_{y,n} + H_{y,\,n+N_xN_y} + H_{z,n} - H_{z,\,n+N_x}
\end{equation}
Even if the H field components are allocated in the dual cell, the $\mathbf{H}$ components are indexed on the primal grid according to the staggering convention. However, notice how the signs are inverted compared to the primal curl, resembling a counter clockwise circulation. This evidences a key algebraic property of the
\gls{fit} discretization, is that the dual curl matrix $\tilde{C}$ is simply the transpose of the primal curl matrix:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}
=
\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{T}}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & \mathbf{P}_z^{\mathrm{T}} & -\mathbf{P}_y^{\mathrm{T}} \\
-\mathbf{P}_z^{\mathrm{T}} & 0 & \mathbf{P}_x^{\mathrm{T}} \\
\mathbf{P}_y^{\mathrm{T}} & -\mathbf{P}_x^{\mathrm{T}} & 0
\end{pmatrix}.
\label{eq:C_dual_matrix}
\end{equation}

This relation is not an approximation but an exact consequence of the primal--dual topology. Going back to the MGE \ref{} the primal curl maps edge quantities $\mathbf{e}$ to face (flux) quantities $\mathbf{b}$: each row computes the circulation around one primal face, while each column corresponds to one primal edge. On the dual grid, $\mathbf{h}$ is stored on dual edges and indexed as primal faces, while $\mathbf{d}$ is stored on dual faces and indexed as primal edges. Thus, the dual curl naturally exchanges the roles of faces and edges, making it the transpose of the primal curl operator.

\subsubsection*{Divergence operator}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures_ch3/fig_divergence.pdf}
\caption{Schematic of the divergence operation on a primal and dual cell.}
\label{fig:yee_divergence}
\end{figure}

The discrete divergence operator is obtained by applying Gauss's law in integral form to each cell, summing the signed normal flux
components through all six faces. Applying it to the volume $V_n$ of the primal cell $n$, the net outward flux of $\mathbf{H}$ through its faces involves the six
$\mathbf{H}$ components sitting at the face centres, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:yee_divergence}:
\begin{equation}
(\mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{h})_n = - H_{x,n} + H_{x,\,n+1} - H_{y,n} + H_{y,\,n+N_x} - H_{z,n} + H_{z,\,n+N_x N_y}.
\end{equation}
Assembling this for all $N$ primal cells gives the primal divergence matrix of size $\{N \times 3N \}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{S} = \bigl(\mathbf{P}_x\;\big|\;\mathbf{P}_y\;\big|\;\mathbf{P}_z\bigr),
\label{eq:S_matrix}
\end{equation}
which maps the $3N$-vector of $\mathbf{H}$ components to an $N$-vector of net fluxes, one per primal cell. The (non-existent) magnetic charges would be allocated to the nodes of the dual grid $\tilde{G}$, located at the center of the primal grid where S is applied. Enforcing
$\mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{D}_A\,\mathbf{b} = 0$ ensures that the discrete magnetic flux is divergence-free by construction.

The dual divergence operator $\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}$ acts on the dual cells $\tilde{n}$ in the same fashion, now accumulating the net
outward flux of $\mathbf{E}$ through the six faces of each dual cell volume $\tilde{V}_n$. From Fig.~\ref{fig:yee_divergence}:
\begin{equation}
(\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}\,\mathbf{e})_{\tilde{n}} = - E_{x,n} + E_{x,\,n+1} - E_{y,n} + E_{y,\,n+N_x} - E_{z,n} + E_{z,\,n+N_x N_y},
\end{equation}
yielding the dual divergence matrix:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\mathbf{S}} =
\bigl(\mathbf{P}_x^t\;\big|\;\mathbf{P}_y^t\;\big|\;\mathbf{P}_z^t\bigr).
\end{equation}
The equation $\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}\,\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_A\,\left(\dfrac{\partial \mathbf{d}}{\partial t}+\mathbf{j}\right)=q$ expresses
the discrete charge continuity equation on the dual grid. This equation dictates that the (possibly existing) electric charges $q$ are defined on nodes of the primal grid $G$, placed at the center of the dual cell volume $\tilde{V}_n$.

\subsection{Divergence conservation}

An important property of the Finite Integration Technique is that charge conservation (also referred to as divergence conservation) is an intrinsic consequence of the discrete topological operators rather than an additional constraint imposed on the numerical solution. This property follows directly from the exact incidence relations of the primal--dual cell complex. Here's a short demonstration for the reader

Starting from the discrete Ampère--Maxwell law,

\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{\mathbf{C}} \, \widehat{\mathbf{h}}
    =
    \frac{d\widehat{\mathbf{d}}}{dt}
    +
    \widehat{\mathbf{j}},
\end{equation}

the dual divergence operator is applied to both sides,

\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{\mathbf{S}} \,
    \widetilde{\mathbf{C}} \,
    \widehat{\mathbf{h}}
    =
    \widetilde{\mathbf{S}}
    \left(
    \frac{d\widehat{\mathbf{d}}}{dt}
    +
    \widehat{\mathbf{j}}
    \right).
\end{equation}

Following the vector mathematical identity divcurl = 0 
\begin{equation}
    \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{A})=0.
\end{equation}

The product of the dual divergence and dual curl operators, as in the continuous case, vanishes identically because every interior edge is shared by two neighbouring faces with opposite orientation , for both primal and dual quantities:

\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{\mathbf{S}}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}=\mathbf{0}, \quad  \mathbf{S}\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{0}
\end{equation}
This identity is an important property of the FIT formalism and is an exact consequence of the connectivity of the primal--dual topology and therefore holds independently of the mesh geometry and for every simulation timestep, allowing to check for numerical and physical correctness of simulations.

It follows that

\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{\mathbf{S}} \,
    \widetilde{\mathbf{C}} \,
    \widehat{\mathbf{h}}
    =
    \widetilde{\mathbf{S}}
    \left(
    \frac{d\widehat{\mathbf{d}}}{dt}
    +
    \widehat{\mathbf{j}}
    \right)
    =0.
\end{equation}

Since the divergence operator is purely topological and independent of time, the time derivative may be exchanged with the divergence operator,

\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{dt}
    \left(
    \widetilde{\mathbf{S}}\widehat{\mathbf{d}}
    \right)
    +
    \widetilde{\mathbf{S}}\widehat{\mathbf{j}}
    =0.
\end{equation}

Finally, invoking the discrete Gauss law, which in MGE form reads

\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{\mathbf{S}}\widehat{\mathbf{d}}
    =
    \widehat{\mathbf{q}},
\end{equation}

yields the discrete continuity equation,

\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \frac{d\widehat{\mathbf{q}}}{dt}
    +
    \widetilde{\mathbf{S}}\widehat{\mathbf{j}}
    =
    0,
    }
\end{equation}

which directly corresponds to the continuous continuity equation,

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}
    +
    \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}
    =
    0.
\end{equation}

Therefore, the conservation of electric charge is exactly preserved by the FIT spatial discretization. No spurious charge can be created or destroyed by the discrete operators, as this property is guaranteed by the underlying topology of the primal--dual grid.  In absence of external source charges, the practical implication is significant: in a time-domain simulation it is sufficient to advance only the two curl equations~\eqref{eq:MGE:faraday} and~\eqref{eq:MGE:ampere} in time, and the divergence constraints are guaranteed to be preserved without any additional enforcement or correction step.
