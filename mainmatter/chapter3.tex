\section{Beam-coupling impedance computation challenges}
% Brief recap of the state of the art + statement of current solver's limitations
In this section, the state-of-the art of wakefield and impedance simulations will be discussed, and the present computation challenges will
be explained. This challenges, together with current solver's limitations, will expose the motivation to build a new wakefield solver, that
within the open-source framework will allow to address present and future impedance simulation requirements.

\subsection{State of the art of impedance simulations}

The evaluation of beam-coupling impedance and wakefields are critical in the design and operation of particle accelerators. As charged
particle beams traverse accelerator structures, interactions with geometric discontinuities and material inhomogeneities generate \gls{em}
fields---known as wakefields---that can degrade beam stability, cause beam-induced heating, and limit machine performance
\cite{metral_impedance_2020}. The frequency-domain counterpart of these interactions is the beam-coupling impedance, an intrinsic property
of each component used to quantify wakefield effects. In order to correcly predict impedance effects on beam stability and on machine
heating, an accurate, fullwave (meaning both long-range narrowband impedance resonances and short-range broadband sources coming from
resistive wall) evaluation of the beam-coupling impedance of the main accelerator components is required.

Analytical methods provide accurate impedance predictions for simplified structures such as resistive walls \cite{yokoya_resistive_1993,
migliorati_resistive_2019}, RF cavities \cite{hofmann_analytic_1990, jensen_rf_2014}, and transitions such as tapers or steps
\cite{yokoya_impedance_1990, stupakov_low_2007, palumbo_wake_1995}. However, for realistic accelerator components with complex geometries,
the full three-dimensional solution of Maxwell's equations \cite{maxwell_viii_1997} is required.

Maxwell's equations can be solved numerically in either frequency or time domain, and several discretization approaches have been developed.
Among the most widely used methods are the \gls{fdtd} method, the \gls{fem}, and the \gls{fvtd} method. These techniques differ primarily in
the formulation of the governing equations (differential or weak form), the choice of basis functions, and the handling of material
interfaces and boundary conditions.

In the frequency domain, for lossless and source-free structures, the problem reduces to an eigenmode formulation, as used in CST Eigenmode,
HFSS, or ACE3P's Omega3P. In contrast, time-domain solvers offer full-wave impedance characterization by directly computing Maxwell's
Equations in integral (see Eqs.~\eqref{eq:Maxwell:a}--\eqref{eq:Maxwell:d}) or differential form - the latter commonly known as Finite
Difference Time Domain (FDTD) method. Commercial solvers such as CST Wakefield, GDFiDL, and closed-source ECHO3D~\cite{zagorodnov_echo4d}
and PBCI~\cite{Lau:1182975} report to use the \gls{fit}
\cite{weiland_finite_2003}, while ACE3P's T3P employs the \gls{fem} \cite{candel_ace3p_2010}.

Several 3D open-source solvers implement full-wave time-domain methods, such as Meep
\cite{oskooi_meep_2010} and WarpX \cite{fedeli_pushing_2022}, both based on the
\gls{fdtd} method \cite{yee_numerical_1966}, and EMcLaW \cite{moreno_emclaw_2021},
which uses a \gls{fvtd} Godunov scheme. However, these tools lack key features needed for beam-coupling impedance calculations--namely;
native \gls{cad} geometry support, advanced material modeling, or wakefield-tailored post-processing.

Therefore, in the field of beam-coupling impedance simulations in time-domain, there is, at the time of this thesis, no open-source
alternative capable of treating arbitrarily shaped 3D accelerator components and provide a fullwave, long-range converged impedance result.
In addition to this, the field is facing increasing challenges when it comes to performing simulations with the currently available tools.

\subsection{Challenges from future accelerator projects}
% Introduction to FCC project and Muon collider project

Despite a more than 30 years long tradition and development of Wakefield solvers, impedance evaluation remains a challenging topic. This
challenges are brought to the surface even more in the regime of future accelerator projects, that push the boundaries of current particle
accelerator designs. At CERN, among the different alternatives for the LHC and HL-LHC successor, two main projects have been discussed.

Considered CERN's first option as a collider successor is the Future Circular Collider (FCC) project. The FCC is a proposed ~91 km circular
collider to be built at CERN, succeeding the LHC in a much larger tunnel beneath the Geneva region. It is planned in two stages: FCC-ee, an
electron-positron collider for precision Higgs, electroweak, and top-quark physics starting in the 2040s, followed by FCC-hh, a
proton-proton collider reaching energies around 100 TeV to directly search for new physics beyond the Standard Model.

A more long-term and first of its kind collider is the Muon collider project, part of the (part of the broader International Muon Collider
Collaboration, IMCC). The Muon Collider Project is a design study for a future particle collider that would smash muons and anti-muons
together at multi-TeV energies. Because muons are much heavier than electrons, they lose far less energy to synchrotron radiation, making a
compact, high-energy lepton collider possible — but their short (2.2 microsecond) lifetime demands extremely fast production, cooling, and
acceleration.

\subsubsection*{FCC impedance challenges}
% Cite FCC papers by Chiara and Carlo
One of the current challenges, in particular for future high energy colliders such as the FCC, is the challenging design parameters, already
in the electron positron FCC-ee collider regime. The simulation of accelerator components for FCC-ee is heavily computationally demanding
due to the short design bunch length of 4 to 12 mm. As explained in Chapter 2, the bunch length determines the frequency range excited by
the passing bunch in Wakefield simulations. The shorter the bunch length, the wider the frequency spectrum excited, following Fourier
Transform principles. Since this, very short, passing bunch needs to be resolved by the simulation mesh (as a rule of thumb, by at least 20
cells per wavelength), this leaves simulation mesh cells in the longitudinal direction of bellow a mm, in accelerator components that can
reach the length of meters.

Furthermore, accelerator components are often designed with highly conductive materials (i.e., Copper) to reduce their impedance. This means
slowly decaying wakefields, that increase the needed simulation length (or wakelength, in the Wakefield jargon) to the Km range in order to
fully resolve the amplitude of impedance resonances. Finally, the design beam pipe aperture is quite large with respect to the design bunch
length. This has a critical consequence: some electromagnetic modes excited by the passing bunch will no longer stay resonating in the
accelerator device, but will be able to travel and propagate through the beam pipe above the so-called cutoff frequency of the beampipe.
This includes a new challenge, the possibility of cross-talk between accelerator components, which would require full control of the
solver's open-boundary conditions implemented to simulate and understand such complex challenge.

The FCC has another key design aspect to take into account: the machine is being designed to have very high beam current, with high number
of bunches and bunch population (11200 and 2.17e11 p/b, respectively, for the Z-mode, at the time of the writing of this manuscript). This
regime, together with the design huge machine circumference of 91 km, increases the accuracy demands for the calculation of beam-coupling
impedances due to tight constrains in terms of beam stability and beam-induced heating.

\subsubsection*{Muon Collider Impedance challenges}
% Cite Elias and Erik's papers

Continuing with future accelerator projects, the Muon Collider also adds new challenges to Wakefield simulations, that cannot be handled in
an straightforward way by current commercial and non commercial available solvers. One example is the so-called impedance in matter, a new
regime for wakefield and impedance that needs to be studied within the Muon Collider project.

During ionization cooling, muons propagate inside matter rather than through vacuum, creating the need to further extend analytical
formalism. This formalism, based on a field matching technique [CITE], has been recently extended to include material-filled geometries
through which the beam can traverse through. The formalism predicts that the presence of impedance resonances with a predictable periodicity
when the beam travserses a dielectric absorber material region. This resonances are attenuated if conductive losses are included in the
model, but remain a significant impedance contribution compared to the only-vacuum case [CITE].

This extended analytical formalism needs to be validated through numerical simulations. However, current commercial solvers do not offer the
possibility to have the beam current traversing materials other than vacuum. One particular case is the design of Muon collider RF cavities
in the muon cooling channel. The copper RF cavities include beryllium windows in the longitudinal direction at the cavity entrance and exit.
The goal of this absorbing windows is to perform a 6D ionization cooling in order to reduce the muon emittance by several orders of
magnitude, while the RF cavities are there to restore the longitudinal momentum lost in the absorbers [CITE].

This regime is challenging both due to the presence of the absorbers, and the fact that the beam cannot be considered ultra-relativistic.
Since the muon relativistic factor $\beta$ ranges from 0.88 to 0.92, the total impedance simulated will include the space charge (SC)
contribution, which needs to be separated from the cavity-driven impedance. Simulations of non-ultra relativistic beam are challenging and
only very advance Wakefield solvers such as CST Wakefield solver can perform such simulations. However, this isolation of the SC
contribution to impedance represent another challenge that current electromagnetic solvers do not provide.

\subsection{Challenges in present particle accelerators}
% Cite Chiaras paper and me & Leo's IPAC paper and me & Carlo's HB paper

Not only new accelerators pose challenges to beam-coupling impedance simulations. As described in Chapter 2, present high energy and high
intensity particle accelerators suffer from impedance-driven component damage that can cause machine downtime and instrumentation failure,
as is the case for the LHC and its accelerator injector chain. As the beam current increases and new intensities are reached, more
impedance-driven problems arise. Therefore, being able to understand, model and predict impedance-driven element failure is essential to
operate accelerators at such demanding regimes, especially in the coming HL-LHC era.

A recent example are the SPS BWS failure, that caused the sublimation of the 4 BWS measurement wires overnight due to high intensity
operation. The problem had to be treated with urgency, with a new design developed based on Wakefield simulations that focused on reducing
the beam-induced heating on the wire element.

Another recent example is the LHC Warm Vacuum interconnecting modules. These modules include thin, highly conductive fingers that ensure the
electromagnetic continuity between the accelerator beampipe and the element transition tube, shielding the corrugated bellow structure
behind them. The bellows are needed to accomodate thermal stress driven expansions and compressions in the accelerator, but the contribution
to the impedance is too large to leave unshielded. However, this thin elements are susceptible to change overtime due to external factors,
ending in a configuration far from the stipulated design. This non-conformities, increase the impedance and therefore the beam induced
heating on the device. This beam-induced heating can likely be non-uniform over the device's individual components. If due to the
composition of the different resonant modes, the heating is localized, it can lead to the damage or in the case of the LHC Warm Vacuum
modules, the melting and plasticizing of some components, in an effect known as the impedance loop. New impedances can lead to higher
beam-induced power loss on the device, causing thermomechanical stresses that further change (and likely worsen) the device's impedance.

\subsection{Key features of next-generation Wakefield solvers}

So far, fullwave impedance simulations of 3D accelerator components with arbitrary geometry and conductive or dispersive materials has only
been possible via black box like commerical solvers. Although they've been developed over 30 years, and are well stablished and benchmarked,
their lack of transparency makes them rigid and stuck in time. Beside this, they represent a single point of failure for complex impedance
evaluations. The impedance field is evolving, and demanding a solver capable of being extendable to tackle newly arising impedance
challenges.

In order to develop CERN's in-house know-how on electromagnetic simulations, In alignment with CERN's open source mission, this project
started to set the stepping stone of a modern Wakefield solver, transparent and accessible to the accelerator community, that can be
enlarged by gathering impedance evaluation expertise from within CERN and different research institutions, by fostering collaboration within
an open source software development framework. It is worth mentioning that this project arrives just when the AI generation in code
development starts to rise. LLM models can help physicists and engineers with little knowledge on code development understand written code
and bridges the transition between user and developer. Open source code can also be easily fed to LLMs to be able to create digital twins of
accelerator components, automatise simulation steps and apply simulation based design modifications faster.

\subsubsection*{Wakis project evolution and roadmap}

Under these premises, the design route for the solver developed in the present thesis, WAKIS, was stablished. The project started as a
post-processing module attached to WarpX FDTD solver, to compute wake potential and impedance from WarpX pre-computed fields [CITE ipac22].
Since WarpX did not include the material tensors, and the beam current had to be modeled via PIC current deposition of millions of
macroparticles, (which is not exactly very computationally friendly), plus WarpX open-boundaries (adjusted PML [CITE J LUC]) showed imporant
limitations when the beam was injected or modes were excited above cutoff, the idea of starting a new solver from scratch arouse and gave
birth to this PhD topic.

Being able to improve and add new physics models to tackle e.g impedance in matter and element cross-talk, the aforementioned impedance
challenges highligths the high demands on computational memory to achieve an extremely fine and accurate mesh to describe all the relevant
geometry details, and calculation speed to be able to conclude these simulations in reasonable time and be able to iterate on the
accelerator components design. This is why, in this thesis work, one of the focuses was on computational speed and memory management, in
particular making the implementation GPU compatible -- a first timer in the field of Wakefield simulations.

In order to produce the stepping stone of a Wakefield solver, the numerical method chosen to follow is the one trusted by the commercial
solvers that have proven to be the most accurate so far for impedance evaluations: The Finite Integration Technique (FIT). This choice is
also motivated by the fact that there is no available open-source solver that implements this numerical formalism. The FIT formalism
discretizes Maxwell's equations in integral form, translating circulation and volume intregrals into large sparse matrix operators. This has
the crucial advantage over loop-based FDTD of being continous in memory and therefore GPU computation friendly. Furthermore, the addition of
embedded boundaries that will delimit different material region areas in the computational domain becomes more stratghtforward, since the
numerical formulation includes rank-3 material tensors. Finally, the addition of the passing beam current, crucial for Wakefield
simulations, reduces to adding an external field current source as a time-dependent initial condition.

Figure \ref{fig:Wakisfeatures} shows the identified key features that our next-generation Wakefield solver needs to have and the progress
achieved during this PhD thesis work. In order to progress in the field of impedance simulations, a modern solver needs to be open-source
and completely transparent to the user, allowing for continuous improvement and extendability. Among the most promising open source options
WarpX[CITE] and EMcLAW[CITE], do not include (or they are not mature enough) some of the fundamental features to perform wakefield
simulations of 3D accelerator components, such as CAD geometry import, material losses, the injection of the beam excitation and the
computation of long-range wakefields. The closed-source code ECHO4D represents a great alternative for short-range wakefield simulations,
with a very solid CAD import from STL formatted geometry [CITE]. Thanks to the co-moving window and the multithreading capabilities, it is
computationally very efficient, even if it is not GPU accelerated. However, does not include material losses and the co-moving window
approach is not meant for long-range wakefield simulations [CITE]. Among the commercial solvers CST Wakefield and Gdfidl, the missing key
features are the GPU acceleration and a transparent Python interface for the user. In particular Gdfidl forces a uniform mesh and also uses
the co-moving window approach, meant for short-range wakefield simulations. From the table, it is clear that the most complete option is CST
Wakefield solver, including most of the required fundamental features. It is the most stablished and benchmarked solver, and the go-to
choice at CERN.

The proposed new Wakefield solver Wakis developed in this thesis, has been developed with these key features in mind, achieving great
progress in most of them during this 3-year development period. Built mainly in Python and benefiting from open-source and trusted Python
libraries such as Numpy and Scipy, it showcases not only the feasibility of developing such tool, but also the future perspectives of
improvement and surpassing stagnated commercial options.
